Ten concepts in plain words

TermSay it like this
Health anxietyPersistent worry about having a serious illness, kept going by misreading normal body sensations and seeking reassurance.
CyberchondriaSearching online about health so much, or so repeatedly, that it makes you more worried, not less.
Correlation rHow closely two scores move together, from 0 (none) to 1 (perfect). 0.47 is moderate: clear, but far from identical.
Partial correlationThe same link after removing the effect of age, gender, background and the rest. Ours barely changed (0.48).
FDR correctionWhen you run many tests, some come out “significant” by luck. FDR adjusts for this; findings that do not survive are called exploratory.
Adjusted odds ratioHow many times the odds of an outcome change for one unit of a factor, holding the others constant. 3.12 = odds about triple.
AUCHow well a model tells two groups apart: 0.5 = coin toss, 1 = perfect. 0.83 (0.79 after correcting for over-fitting) is good.
MediationSplitting a link into a part that goes through a third variable and a direct part. Statistical, not proof of cause.
CFAChecks that questionnaire items group into the factors they are supposed to measure (e.g., the four CSS facets).
Measurement invarianceChecks that the questionnaire measures the same thing in Arabic and English, so the two groups can be compared fairly.
Never say (it overclaims, and the committee will catch it):
  • “Health anxiety causes cyberchondria” → say “is associated with”, “is compatible with a cycle”.
  • “19% have health anxiety disorder” → say “19% scored in the elevated range on a screening tool”.
  • “AI makes people more anxious” → our data show no reliable independent effect of AI.
  • “Our results apply to all of Baghdad” → the sample is a young, online, convenience sample.

  • 1.5: why AI chatbots are different from search engines (conversational, repeatable, empathetic-sounding).

  • Figure 1 (conceptual framework) and Figure 2 (study flowchart): you present the study model and the study flow.
  • 2.1–2.5: design, sampling, inclusion criteria, sample size calculation, the five parts of the questionnaire, the SHAI ≥ 18 definition and why it is called “elevated”.
  • 2.6 at headline level: you name the methods; Student 2 explains them in detail.

Your numbers (know them without looking)

  • Data collection 17 July 2026 – 11 September 2026; n = 300
  • Sample size 194 for r = 0.20 (α 0.05, 80% power); margin of error ±5.7%
  • SHAI-14: 14 items, 0–42; cut-off 18 (Swedish study); CSS-12: 12 items, 12–60, four facets
  • Iraq: > 80% online; Iraqi COVID survey: about half reported health anxiety

Questions you should be ready for

Q: Why did you choose this topic?

Give your real reason (what you noticed in patients, family or yourselves using ChatGPT before seeing a doctor). Then: no Iraqi data, and AI chatbots had not been studied next to search engines.

Q: Why cross-sectional and not longitudinal?

Feasible within a student project; it measures associations. We say clearly that it cannot show direction, and we recommend longitudinal studies.

Q: Convenience sampling is biased. Why use it?

No sampling frame for online adults in Baghdad. We describe the sample honestly, avoid generalising percentages, and show associations were consistent across subgroups.

Q: How did you calculate the sample size?

Fisher's z for a correlation: r = 0.20, α = 0.05, 80% power gives 194; we collected 300. Meta-analytic r is ≈ 0.62, so this is conservative.

Q: Why SHAI ≥ 18?

It best separated people with health anxiety from healthy controls in a Swedish diagnostic study. It is a screening threshold, so we say “elevated”, and we report other cut-offs (only 0.7% at 29).

Q: Is your Arabic version validated?

Not by formal back-translation, which is a limitation. But we tested it: equal reliability, and formal invariance tests showed the CSS-12 works identically in Arabic and English; the SHAI-14 had equal loadings.

Q: How did you make sure participants lived in Baghdad?

Through Baghdad-based distribution channels; there was no residence question, and we list this as a limitation.

Q: Do you have ethical approval?

Give the committee name, number and date (fill this in the thesis before the defence).

Your numbers (know them without looking)

  • Facets: excessiveness 0.46, distress 0.45, compulsion 0.25, reassurance 0.24
  • R²: 0.030 → 0.133 → 0.317 (health anxiety adds 18.4%); β = 0.48; B = 0.68 CSS points per SHAI point
  • Logistic: AOR 3.12 (cyberchondria per SD), 3.83 (reassurance motive), 0.61 (age); AUC 0.83 → 0.79
  • Mediation: indirect 0.16 (CI 0.11–0.23); via distress 0.22, direct 0.01; reverse 0.15

Questions you should be ready for

Q: Why non-parametric tests?

The scores were not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk). We also ran parametric tests (Welch) as confirmation; conclusions were the same.

Q: What is FDR and why use it?

We ran many tests; FDR controls the share of false positives among significant results. What did not survive is labelled exploratory.

Q: Explain r = 0.47 to a non-statistician.

People with more health anxiety tend clearly to have more cyberchondria; anxiety explains about a fifth (22%) of the differences in cyberchondria.

Q: Why hierarchical regression?

To show how much health anxiety adds after demographics and digital use: 18.4% extra variance, the biggest block by far.

Q: Does mediation prove a vicious cycle?

No. The data are from one time point and the reverse direction fits too. It is compatible with a cycle; only longitudinal data can show direction.

Q: Why does the AI effect disappear?

It depends on 32 people who named a chatbot but did not tick the box; they had the highest scores. With the strict definition, p = 0.31. And only 34 people were non-users, a small comparison group.

Q: Chronic illness was not significant in the logistic model. Why?

Turning SHAI into yes/no (≥ 18) loses information and power. On the continuous SHAI score, chronic illness is significant: p = 0.004 unadjusted, and still p = 0.029 in the adjusted linear model (B = +1.7 points).

Q: What do CFI and RMSEA mean?

Fit indices for CFA: CFI near 0.95 or above and RMSEA below about 0.08 indicate acceptable fit. CSS-12 four factors: CFI 0.95, RMSEA 0.07.

Q: What is the AUC, and why is it corrected?

How well the model separates elevated from non-elevated anxiety. A model always looks better on its own data, so we corrected for this by bootstrapping: 0.83 → 0.79.

When you edit your chapter

  • Results are mostly numbers: do not change any number, p-value, CI or test name. Rewrite only the connecting sentences.
  • Keep “Table n” and “Figure n” mentions; never type caption numbers by hand (they are automatic fields).
  • If a sentence seems wrong or unclear, do not “fix” the statistics yourself: mark it with a comment and ask.
  • You will be asked about statistics in the Method too: read 2.6 until you can explain each method in one sentence.

  • 4.4–4.5: AI chatbots (why our AI use is so much higher than in US surveys) and the consultation (delay, non-disclosure, trust).

Your numbers (know them without looking)

  • Our r 0.47 vs meta-analyses 0.62–0.63; corrected for reliability ≈ 0.56
  • AI use: ours 78–89% vs ≈ 20% (US 2024) and 21% (2025); 96% at 18–19 y vs 72% at ≥ 40 y
  • 34.7% delay care when reassured; 31.7% never tell the doctor; 34.7% say trust was strained
  • Egypt: 1435 medical students, 85.8% moderate cyberchondria
  • ClinicalTrials.gov: only 9 cyberchondria studies, none in Iraq

Questions you should be ready for

Q: Why is your correlation weaker than other studies?

Measurement error (corrected r ≈ 0.56), low and narrow health anxiety in our sample, online recruitment of confident users, and the short CSS-12 instead of the 33-item version.

Q: Is your sample representative of Baghdad?

No, and we say so: young, educated, many medical students. Percentages should not be generalised; associations were consistent across subgroups.

Q: What is new in your study?

First CSS-12 data from Baghdad; AI chatbots studied separately from search engines; psychology linked to real behaviour before the consultation.

Q: Why is AI use so high in your sample?

A young, educated, health-oriented sample; AI use fell clearly with age. It is not an estimate for all adults.

Q: What is your biggest limitation?

The cross-sectional design: we cannot say what causes what. Second, the convenience sample.

Q: If you repeated the study, what would you change?

Random sampling of all ages, formal Arabic adaptation, a clearer AI-use question, and a follow-up to test the cycle over time.

Q: What should a doctor do differently on Monday?

Ask every patient, without judgement, what they searched or asked an AI; recognise repeated searching and reassurance seeking as signs of health anxiety.

Q: Are medical students more anxious?

Unadjusted yes, but it disappeared after adjustment: it reflected their younger age. Their cyberchondria was the same as everyone else's.

When you edit your chapter

  • Discussion is the most “human” chapter: interpret in your own words, add your own clinical reasoning, keep every reference [n].
  • Do not strengthen claims (no “proves”, “causes”). Keep hedges such as “compatible with”, “exploratory”.
  • Recommendations can carry your own voice and local knowledge (Iraqi health system, Arabic resources), as long as they follow from the results.

Making the writing genuinely yours

A thesis you can defend is one you could have written: every sentence is something you understand and would say. That is also what protects you best, whatever tool your college uses to check the text.

The rewrite method (paragraph by paragraph)

  1. Read the paragraph until you understand it. Look up anything you can't explain.
  2. Close it and explain it out loud, as you would to a classmate.
  3. Write your version from that explanation, in your normal academic English.
  4. Compare with the original: same facts, same numbers, same [n] references, nothing claimed more strongly than before.
  5. Read aloud. If you would never say a phrase, change it.

What makes text sound like you

  • Your own reasons and observations: why you chose the topic, what you saw in clinics or among friends, what surprised you in the results. Only true things.
  • Your experience of the study: how you shared the link, how people reacted, what was hard. A short paragraph in the Method or Discussion is fine if true.
  • Local context you know better than any source: how people in Baghdad use ChatGPT, pharmacies, private clinics, the cost of visits.
  • Plain, varied sentences. Cut stock phrases such as “it is important to note”, “plays a crucial role”, “in today's digital age”, “delve into”, “a testament to”.
  • One team voice: after rewriting, one student reads the whole thesis and smooths differences in style.
About AI detectors, honestly. Detectors are unreliable in both directions, and tricks to fool them (paraphrasing tools, deliberate errors, odd synonyms) make the thesis worse and can count as misconduct. I won't help with those. What protects you:
  1. Check your college's AI policy and ask your supervisor what must be declared.
  2. Declare the AI assistance if required. A sentence you can adapt for the Acknowledgment or Method:
    “The statistical analysis code and an initial draft of this report were prepared with the assistance of an AI tool (Claude, Anthropic). All analyses were checked, and the text was reviewed and rewritten by the authors, who take full responsibility for its content.”
  3. Keep your edit history (Track Changes, OneDrive version history, your notes): it shows your own work.
  4. Understand every sentence: the oral defence is the real test, and this guide prepares you for it.

Part 4 · Presenting

Practise like this

  1. Alone: read your speaker notes (in PowerPoint: View → Notes), then present your slides out loud three times, each time using fewer notes.
  2. Time yourself: Student 1 ≈ 5 min, Student 2 ≈ 6 min, Student 3 ≈ 4 min. If over, cut words, not slides.
  3. Together: two full run-throughs with handovers, standing up, with the clicker.
  4. Mock committee: take turns asking each other the Part 2 questions. Answer in under 40 seconds each.
  5. Record one rehearsal on a phone and watch it once: speed, filler words (“umm”, “so basically”), eye contact.

On the day

  • Look at the committee, not the screen. Point to the number you are talking about.
  • One message per slide: say the “In summary…” line from your notes.
  • Speak slower than feels natural; pause after key numbers (r = 0.47, 19%, 78.0%).
  • Stand to one side; the next speaker stands ready before the handover.
  • Bring the deck on a USB stick and as a PDF (in case the PC lacks fonts).

Handling questions

  • Who answers: the owner of that part answers first; statistics → Student 2. Others add only if needed.
  • Pause, repeat the question briefly, answer in two or three sentences, stop.
  • Accept limitations calmly: “Yes, that is a limitation; we addressed it by… and recommend…”.
  • If you don't know: “We did not examine that directly; based on our data we would expect…, and it is a good question for future research.” Never invent a number.
  • If a teammate struggles, help with “If I may add…”.

Handover lines (memorise)

  • Student 1 → 2: “That was our methodology. Now my colleague will present what we found.”
  • Student 2 → 3: “Those are our results. My colleague will now discuss what they mean.”
  • Student 3 → committee: “Thank you for listening; we welcome your questions.” (leave the r = 0.47 slide up)
