1. The study in 60 seconds

Say this if asked "summarise your research":
"Many people now look up their symptoms online, and increasingly they ask AI chatbots like ChatGPT before they see a doctor. We wanted to know how this relates to health anxiety and to cyberchondria, which is online searching that makes worry worse instead of better. We surveyed 300 adults in Baghdad using two validated scales, the SHAI-14 and the CSS-12. We found that AI chatbots are now the main source, used by 88.7%. About 19% had clinically relevant health anxiety. Health anxiety and cyberchondria were moderately correlated, r = 0.47, and health anxiety was the strongest predictor of cyberchondria, especially of excessive searching and distress after searching. The pattern is consistent with a cycle in which anxiety drives searching, searching drives distress, and distress drives extra doctor visits. Searching also shapes the consultation: about a third delay care because they feel reassured, and a third never tell their doctor about their searches. Because the study is cross-sectional, we cannot prove the direction of the cycle."

Aim

To study the relationship between health anxiety and cyberchondria and their correlates among adults in Baghdad who search for health information online, with attention to AI chatbots and pre-consultation behaviour.

Objectives (memorise the gist)

  1. Describe tools, motives and behaviours of online health searching (including AI).
  2. Measure levels of health anxiety and cyberchondria.
  3. Measure the strength of their association, overall and by dimension.
  4. Test every variable's association with both.
  5. Find independent predictors (multivariable models).
  6. Test pathways (mediation) and modifiers (moderation).
  7. Link both to pre-consultation behaviour, disclosure and trust in doctors.

Hypotheses and verdicts

HypothesisVerdict
H1: Health anxiety is positively associated with cyberchondria, most strongly with distress and excessiveness.Supported (r = 0.47; excessiveness 0.46, distress 0.45)
H2: The association is independent of socio-demographic and digital factors.Supported (partial r = 0.48; β = 0.48)
H3: Excessive searching mediates the link between health anxiety and post-search distress.Supported, partial mediation (indirect 0.16, 95% CI 0.11–0.23). Cross-sectional caveat.
H4: Higher cyberchondria goes with care-seeking and a strained doctor relationship.Partly supported: urgent visits and strained relationship yes; multiple doctors not independently; most behaviour results exploratory after FDR.

2. Background concepts

Health anxiety

A continuum from normal worry to illness anxiety disorder (DSM-5). The cognitive-behavioural model (Salkovskis & Warwick, 1986) says people misinterpret normal body sensations as signs of serious disease; checking and reassurance-seeking reduce anxiety briefly but keep it going, because the person never learns the feared illness is not there. Lifetime prevalence of health anxiety disorder was 5.7% in an Australian national survey; almost half of Iraqi respondents reported health anxiety during COVID-19 restrictions (Karim et al., 2020).

Cyberchondria

Coined by White & Horvitz (2009): searches for common symptoms escalate towards rare, serious diagnoses. Starcevic & Berle (2013) define it as excessive or repeated online searching that increases distress. Searching itself is normal; it becomes cyberchondria when it is excessive, compulsive and distressing. It is seen as a safety behaviour maintained by intermittent reinforcement (sometimes the search reassures, so the habit is reinforced).

What was known before

  • Meta-analyses: health anxiety correlates r ≈ 0.62–0.63 with cyberchondria and r ≈ 0.34 with general online searching (McMullan 2019; Schenkel 2021).
  • One large longitudinal Dutch study (te Poel 2016) found a reciprocal loop in non-clinical health anxiety but not in clinical health anxiety.
  • Almost no data from Iraq, and AI chatbots had not been studied alongside search engines.

If asked "why non-parametric tests?": "The Shapiro–Wilk test showed the scores were not perfectly normal, and several subscales were skewed, so we used rank-based tests for group comparisons. For regression we used robust (HC3) standard errors and bootstrap intervals, and we also ran Welch's parametric tests as a check; the conclusions were the same."
If asked "why FDR correction?": "Because we tested every variable against every outcome, more than 160 tests. At p < 0.05 we would expect about 8 false positives by chance, so we controlled the false discovery rate and labelled anything that did not survive as exploratory."

5. Results walkthrough

5.1 Sample

300 adults; 62.3% aged 20–29; 56.3% female; 44.0% medical students; 63.3% health-related background; 21.7% chronic condition; 70.7% answered mainly in Arabic.

The sample is young and dominated by medical students: say so before the committee does.

5.2 How people search

Figure 1 of the thesis: tools and motivations.
What to say: "AI chatbots have overtaken search engines: 88.7% use them versus 56.3% for search engines, and 41.0% use AI without any search engine. ChatGPT dominates. People search mainly for convenience, to decide whether they need a doctor, and for instant reassurance."

5.3 What they do after searching

Figure 2 of the thesis: behaviour, disclosure and relationship.
What to say: "The most common response is to delay or skip care because the search reassured them (34.7%). Others go the opposite way: 22.0% seek urgent care and 25.3% see several doctors. Only 27.0% always tell their doctor what they found; 31.7% never do, for fear of being judged. And 34.7% feel searching has complicated their relationship with doctors."

5.4 Levels of health anxiety and cyberchondria

SHAI mean 12.3 ± 5.8; 19% ≥ 18 (95% CI 15.0–23.8%). CSS mean 28.9 ± 8.4. Excessiveness highest, compulsion lowest: people search a lot, but it rarely disrupts their lives.

5.5 The main result: health anxiety and cyberchondria

Figure 3 of the thesis: SHAI vs CSS.
What to say: "This is our main finding. Health anxiety and cyberchondria are moderately correlated, r = 0.47 (95% CI 0.38–0.55). The association hardly changes when we adjust for everything else (partial r = 0.48). It is strongest for excessive searching and distress after searching."
Figure 8 of the thesis: CSS subscales by health-anxiety status.

5.6 Which items drive the link

Figure 9 (appendix): item-by-item correlations.
What to say: "When we looked item by item, 111 of 168 pairs were significant after correction. The strongest links involve fear of serious illness and interpreting sensations, for example fear of illness with panic when reading about a rare disease. Simply noticing aches and sensations was not linked. So it is the catastrophic interpretation, not body awareness, that drives searching."

5.7 Who is affected (bivariate)

Health anxiety higher in: younger respondents, students, chronic illness, social-media searchers, and reassurance seekers. Gender: no difference. Cyberchondria: almost unrelated to demographics; higher in AI users and in those combining AI + search engines.

Figure 5 of the thesis: cyberchondria by tool profile.

5.8 Independent predictors

Cyberchondria model: demographics R² 0.030; + digital use 0.133; + health anxiety 0.317 (ΔR² 18.4%). SHAI B = 0.68 per point (β = 0.48). AI use B = +5.3 (95% CI 2.3–8.3).

Clinically relevant health anxiety (logistic): cyberchondria AOR 3.12 per SD; reassurance motive AOR 3.83; each older age group AOR 0.61; AUC 0.83.

Figure 6 of the thesis: adjusted odds ratios for SHAI ≥ 18.

5.9 Pathways (mediation)

Figure 7 of the thesis: mediation models.
What to say: "We tested your question of whether anxiety and searching feed each other. Excessive searching carries part of the link between health anxiety and distress (indirect effect 0.16). Distress after searching carries almost all of the link between health anxiety and reassurance-seeking visits; the direct effect drops to 0.01. The reverse ordering is also significant (0.15). This is what a two-way cycle would look like, but with one-time data we cannot prove which comes first."

5.10 Behaviour models

Higher cyberchondria: more urgent visits (AOR 1.51), less ignoring (AOR 0.65). Disclosure paradox: SHAI AOR 1.47 for never disclosing, CSS AOR 0.57. Higher trust: fewer multiple-doctor visits (AOR 0.59). Neither score predicts self-medication or reassured delay. Only the disclosure finding survives FDR; the rest are exploratory.

5.11 Moderation and robustness

No moderator changed the HA–CSS link (trust, AI use, background, gender, chronic disease, language, age). Medical students r = 0.57 vs others 0.40 (not significantly different). Excluding flagged or straight-line responders: r unchanged (0.46 / 0.47).

6. Interpreting the findings

  • Why is our r (0.47) lower than meta-analyses (~0.62)? Low-anxiety sample with a narrow range; young, digitally habitual users for whom searching is routine; most earlier studies used the 33-item CSS, which overlaps more with health anxiety.
  • Fear, not vigilance: interpretation and fear items drive cyberchondria, which fits the cognitive model of health anxiety.
  • AI chatbots: a new channel rather than a proven new cause. Conversational, empathetic answers may invite "one more question". The effect is sensitive to classification (32 inconsistent answers; only 34 non-users).
  • Two opposite behaviours: searching pushes some people to complacency (delay, ignore) and others to over-use (urgent visits, multiple doctors). The reassured delay happens regardless of anxiety level, which is a safety concern.
  • Concealment paradox: health-anxious people hide searching (fear of being labelled "worried well"), while heavy searchers bring findings to the doctor as reassurance seeking. Clinicians must ask.
  • Medical students: higher health anxiety only before adjustment (an age effect); identical cyberchondria to others, so medical knowledge does not protect.

10. Anticipated committee questions with model answers

About the design and sample

Q. Why a cross-sectional design?
It is the most feasible design for a graduation project and suitable for measuring associations. We are clear that it cannot establish causality; a longitudinal design is recommended.
Q. How do you know participants were from Baghdad?
The link was distributed only through Baghdad-based online groups. We did not include a residence question, which we acknowledge as a limitation.
Q. Your sample is mostly medical students. Is that a problem?
It limits generalisability of percentages such as AI use and prevalence. But the main association was the same across all occupational groups, medical students did not differ in cyberchondria, and we adjusted for occupation in all models.
Q. How did you calculate the sample size?
For the primary objective, a correlation: detecting r = 0.20 with 80% power and α = 0.05 requires 194. With 300 we had 94% power, and the smallest detectable correlation was 0.16. For proportions, the margin of error is ±5.7%.
Q. Why did you include participants who answered in English?
Many students in Baghdad study in English. Offering both languages improved accessibility. We checked that both language groups responded equivalently.

About the instruments

Q. Why the SHAI-14 and the CSS-12?
They are the most widely used validated measures of each construct, they are short (good for online surveys), the CSS-12 was validated against the SHAI, and both have Arabic versions in the literature.
Q. Were the scales validated in Arabic?
Arabic versions exist (Alshayea 2020 for the SHAI; Hallit 2023 for the CSS-12). We used our own wording, so we tested reliability and structure in our data: α 0.81 and 0.86, four-factor CSS structure confirmed, and equivalent results for Arabic and English responders.
Q. Why a cut-off of 18?
It was the optimal SHAI-14 cut-off for distinguishing health anxiety from healthy controls in a 2022 diagnostic-accuracy study. It is an indicator, not a diagnosis, so we also report 15 and 22 and use the continuous score.
Q. What about the answer-order differences on the form?
Items were scored by the meaning of the statement chosen, following the original scoring key, so the order did not affect scores.

About the analysis

Q. Why both Pearson and Spearman?
Pearson is standard and comparable with meta-analyses; Spearman is robust to the mild non-normality. Both gave 0.47.
Q. What does r = 0.47 mean in practice?
A moderate positive association: people with more health anxiety tend to have more cyberchondria. Health anxiety accounts for about 22% of the differences in cyberchondria.
Q. What is FDR and why use it?
False discovery rate correction controls the proportion of false positives when running many tests. We tested every variable, so without it some significant results would be chance findings.
Q. What is mediation and what did you find?
It tests whether X is linked to Y through M. Excessive searching partly carries the link from health anxiety to distress (indirect 0.16). Distress almost fully carries the link from health anxiety to reassurance-seeking visits.
Q. Can mediation in cross-sectional data prove a cycle?
No. It tests whether the data are consistent with a proposed ordering. We also tested the reverse ordering, which was also significant, which is consistent with a two-way relationship but cannot prove direction.
Q. Why robust (HC3) standard errors?
The residuals showed some heteroskedasticity, and robust standard errors remain valid in that situation.
Q. Did you check multicollinearity?
Yes: the highest VIF was 3.9, below the usual threshold of 5–10.
Q. How robust are the results?
Excluding the three annotated responses, excluding nine straight-line responders, bootstrapping, and changing the cut-off did not change the main results. An independent analysis of the same data reached the same conclusions.

About the findings

Q. Is AI making people more anxious?
We cannot say that. AI users scored higher on cyberchondria (+5.3 points), but with a strict definition of AI use the effect disappears (B = 1.5, p = 0.31), and the non-user group was small (34). AI is best described as a new channel for the same behaviour.
Q. Why is your correlation lower than in previous studies?
Our sample had relatively low health anxiety (narrow range), was young and digitally habitual, and we used the short CSS-12 rather than the 33-item version.
Q. What is the most clinically important finding?
That a third of people delay care because they feel reassured, and a third never tell their doctor about their searches; the more health-anxious hide it more. Doctors should ask about online and AI searches routinely.
Q. Why do health-anxious people hide their searches?
Probably fear of being labelled as anxious or "worried well", which the answer option itself reflects. Patients with high cyberchondria, in contrast, bring their findings as part of reassurance seeking.
Q. Do medical students have more health anxiety?
Only before adjustment; the difference disappeared after accounting for age. Their cyberchondria was identical to others, so medical knowledge did not protect against it.
Q. What would you do differently?
Use random sampling, add a residence question, formally back-translate and pilot the Arabic version, measure behaviour frequency rather than tick boxes, and follow participants over time.
Q. What is new in your study?
First Iraqi data on health anxiety and cyberchondria; one of the first studies to place AI chatbots alongside search engines; linking both to concrete pre-consultation behaviours and disclosure to doctors.
Q. What are your recommendations?
Clinicians: ask about searches non-judgementally and recognise repeated searching with distress as a sign of health anxiety. Public health: Arabic digital/AI health-literacy campaigns. AI developers: anxiety-aware responses. Researchers: longitudinal studies and formal Arabic validation.

11. Glossary

TermMeaning
Health anxietyPersistent worry about having or developing a serious illness, kept going by misreading normal body sensations and by checking and reassurance-seeking.
CyberchondriaExcessive or repeated online health searching that increases (rather than relieves) anxiety or distress.
SHAI-14Short Health Anxiety Inventory, 14 items each scored 0–3 (total 0–42). Higher = more health anxiety.
CSS-12Cyberchondria Severity Scale short form: 12 items scored 1–5 (total 12–60), four 3-item subscales.

CompulsionCSS subscale: searching interferes with work, leisure and social life.
DistressCSS subscale: panic and anxiety caused by what is read online.

Convenience / snowball samplingNon-random recruitment of whoever is reachable, with participants sharing the link onwards.
Pearson r / Spearman ρCorrelation coefficients from −1 to +1. Spearman uses ranks and does not assume normality. 0.1 small, 0.3 moderate, 0.5 large.
Partial correlationThe correlation that remains after statistically removing the effect of other variables (covariates).
Mann–Whitney U / Kruskal–Wallis HNon-parametric tests comparing two / three-or-more groups on a score.
Chi-square (χ²) / Fisher exactTests whether two categorical variables are associated; Fisher is used when counts are small.
Effect sizeHow big a difference or association is (e.g. r, ε², Cramér's V, β, OR), independent of sample size.
p-valueProbability of a result at least this extreme if there were truly no association. p < 0.05 = "statistically significant".
FDR / q-valueFalse discovery rate correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) for running many tests; q < 0.05 means the finding survives the correction.
Holm–BonferroniA stricter multiple-testing correction controlling the chance of any false positive.
Linear (OLS) regression, B and βPredicts a score from several variables at once. B = change in outcome per unit of predictor; β = the same in standard-deviation units (comparable across predictors).
R² / ΔR²Proportion of variance in the outcome explained by the model / by an added block of variables.
HC3 robust standard errorsStandard errors that stay valid when the spread of residuals is uneven (heteroskedasticity).
Logistic regression, AORPredicts a yes/no outcome. Adjusted odds ratio > 1 = higher odds, < 1 = lower odds, after accounting for other variables.
AUCHow well a model separates people with and without the outcome: 0.5 = chance, 0.8+ = good.
MediationTests whether X is linked to Y through a middle variable M (X → M → Y). Indirect effect = a × b.
BootstrapRe-sampling the data thousands of times to get confidence intervals without strong assumptions.
Moderation / interactionTests whether the strength of an association differs by a third variable (e.g. by gender).
