1The study in full

Title

Health Anxiety, AI Chatbot Use and Cyberchondria Among Adults in Baghdad: An Online Cross-Sectional Survey

Abstract (as in the thesis)

Background: Many adults look up symptoms online before seeing a doctor, increasingly with artificial-intelligence (AI) chatbots. Health anxiety and cyberchondria (excessive, distressing online health searching) are thought to feed each other, but evidence from Iraq, and on AI chatbots, is scarce.

Objectives: To measure the association between health anxiety and cyberchondria among adults in Baghdad, and to describe online and AI health-information seeking and behaviour before consulting a doctor.

Methods: Cross-sectional online survey of 300 adults in Baghdad (July–September 2026) using the Short Health Anxiety Inventory (SHAI-14) and the Cyberchondria Severity Scale (CSS-12), analysed with non-parametric group comparisons, χ² tests, correlation, multiple linear and binary logistic regression, and mediation analysis (Sobel test).

Results: Participants were mostly young (62.3% aged 20–29). AI chatbots were ticked by 78.0% (88.7% including those who named a chatbot), more than search engines (56.3%). Health anxiety correlated with cyberchondria (r = 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.55) and remained its strongest predictor after adjustment (β = 0.48), most strongly with excessive searching and post-search distress. Elevated health anxiety (SHAI-14 ≥ 18, a screening threshold, not a diagnosis) was found in 19.0%. After searching, 34.7% delayed or skipped care because they felt reassured and 31.7% never told their doctor, citing fear of judgement. The association between AI use and cyberchondria was not robust to how AI use was defined.

Conclusion: In this young, health-oriented convenience sample, health anxiety and cyberchondria were linked and AI chatbots were a major source of health information, without reliable evidence that they add to cyberchondria beyond health anxiety. Clinicians should ask patients, without judgement, what they have searched or asked an AI.

Keywords: cyberchondria; health anxiety; artificial intelligence; chatbots; Iraq.

Why the study was done (Rationale, 1.7)

This study addresses three gaps: it gives, to our knowledge, the first estimate of the association between health anxiety and cyberchondria (CSS-12) in Baghdad; it examines AI chatbots separately from search engines; and it links both constructs to what clinicians see before and during a consultation.

Aim, objectives and hypotheses (1.8, word for word)

Aim: To study the relationship between health anxiety and cyberchondria among adults in Baghdad who search for health information online, with a focus on AI chatbots and pre-consultation behaviour.

Objectives:

1. To describe where adults in Baghdad look for health information (online and through AI) and what they do before seeing a doctor.

2. To estimate levels of health anxiety and cyberchondria and the strength of their association, independently of socio-demographic and digital-use factors (H1, H2).

3. To examine statistical pathways consistent with a mutually reinforcing cycle (H3).

4. To examine how health anxiety and cyberchondria relate to pre-consultation behaviour, disclosure of searches and trust in physicians (H4).

Hypotheses: (H1) People with more health anxiety have more cyberchondria, especially on the distress and excessiveness dimensions. (H2) This holds after adjustment for socio-demographic and digital-use factors. (H3) Part of the link between health anxiety and post-search distress runs, statistically, through excessive searching. (H4) People with more cyberchondria make more urgent visits and multiple consultations and report a more strained doctor–patient relationship. H1 and H2 were primary and H3 and H4 secondary; all other analyses were exploratory (Figure 1).

Methods (2.1–2.4, word for word; 2.5 is in Section 7)

This analytical cross-sectional study follows the STROBE [21] and CHERRIES [22] reporting guidelines. Data were collected online in Baghdad between 17 July 2026 and 11 September 2026 by convenience and snowball sampling through Baghdad-based social-media and messaging groups. Adults aged 18 or over who could read Arabic or English and gave informed consent were eligible. All completed submissions were analysed; no duplicate response patterns were found [Authors to state whether Google Forms limited submissions to one per account]. Three responses annotated by the team during data collection [authors to state the reason] were kept and tested in a sensitivity analysis.

2.2 Sample size

Using Fisher's z with two-sided α = 0.05 and 80% power, 194 participants are needed to detect a correlation of r = 0.20 [23]; meta-analytic estimates are far larger (r ≈ 0.62), so the target is conservative. For proportions, 300 participants give a margin of error of ±5.7%.

2.3 Questionnaire

A bilingual (Arabic–English) Google Form had five parts: (1) consent and socio-demographic data; (2) digital health-information seeking (tools, main AI chatbot, motives and trust in online/AI information); (3) the 14-item Short Health Anxiety Inventory (SHAI-14; items scored 0–3, total 0–42) [24], which has good reliability and validity [25,26]; (4) the CSS-12 (items scored 1–5, total 12–60; four 3-item subscales) [8]; and (5) behaviour after searching, disclosure of searches to the physician and the effect on the relationship with physicians. The Arabic wording was written by the team without formal cross-cultural adaptation [27]; published Arabic versions [9,28] were not used. The response options of one SHAI item (item 2) were displayed in a different order from the original and were scored by meaning.

2.4 Definitions

Elevated health anxiety: SHAI-14 ≥ 18, the cut-off that best separated people with health anxiety from healthy controls in a Swedish diagnostic study [26]; it is a screening threshold, not a diagnosis, so prevalence at 15, 22 and 29 is also reported.

High cyberchondria: CSS-12 > 33 (upper tertile), as the CSS-12 has no validated cut-off.

AI-chatbot user: ticked "AI chatbots" or named a chatbot; because 32 respondents named one without ticking it, all AI analyses were repeated with the strict (ticked-only) definition.

Health-related background: medical students, other health-science students and healthcare workers.

Ethics (2.6)

Participation was voluntary and anonymous; electronic informed consent (with confirmation of age ≥ 18) was required before any question, and no names or contact details were collected. [Ethical approval: committee name, approval number and date to be inserted.]

Conclusions (5.1, word for word)

1. AI chatbots, mainly ChatGPT, are a leading source of health information before consulting a doctor among digitally active adults in Baghdad (78.0% ticked; 88.7% including those who named one).

2. About one in five (19%) had elevated health anxiety on a screening threshold; younger people and reassurance seekers were most affected.

3. Health anxiety and cyberchondria are moderately associated (r = 0.47), compatible with a reinforcing cycle that this design cannot prove.

4. There is no reliable evidence that AI use adds to cyberchondria beyond health anxiety.

5. Searching shapes the consultation: about a third delay care when reassured and about a third never disclose their searches; higher cyberchondria goes with more disclosure.

Recommendations (5.2, word for word)

Clinicians: ask every patient, without judgement, what they have searched or asked an AI; treat repeated searching and reassurance seeking as possible signs of health anxiety; teach digital and AI health literacy in medical school.

Ministry of Health: the Ministry may consider Arabic digital and AI health-literacy campaigns that warn about false reassurance, and reliable Arabic triage sources.

AI developers: developers may consider anxiety-aware health answers that avoid escalating rare diagnoses and encourage appropriate consultation.

Research: longitudinal studies, probability samples and formally adapted Arabic scales; intervention trials are needed, as ClinicalTrials.gov lists only nine cyberchondria studies and none in Iraq [36].

Strengths and limitations (4.5, word for word)

Strengths include two established, reliable scales, a bilingual questionnaire, separate assessment of AI chatbots, complete data, adjustment for confounders in multivariable models and sensitivity analyses. The main limitations are:

Design: cross-sectional data cannot establish direction; mediation describes statistical pathways only.

Sample: an online convenience sample, young and with many medical students (44.0%), so percentages cannot be generalised to Baghdad; residence was not verified by a question.

Measurement: Arabic wording without formal back-translation or a confirmatory factor analysis; one SHAI item with reordered options; CSS item 3 mentions only search engines and may miss repeated chatbot use; behaviour, trust and disclosure measured with single, non-validated items; all data self-reported.

Analysis: many statistical tests were run without correction for multiple comparisons, so some significant results may be due to chance; small subgroups (34 AI non-users); about five events per predictor in the logistic model; the AI result depends on how 32 respondents were classified.

“Many people now look up their symptoms online before seeing a doctor, and more and more of them ask AI chatbots like ChatGPT. For some people this searching makes them more worried, not less. 4Statistics from zero (with our numbers)

Each lesson: the idea in plain words, our example, and what to say. Read them in order; each one builds on the one before.

1. Variables: two kinds
  • Categorical = groups (gender, uses AI yes/no, tool profile). We describe them with n and %.
  • Continuous = scores on a scale (SHAI 0–42, CSS 12–60). We describe them with mean, SD, median.

This one fact decides almost every choice of test and chart in the thesis.

2. Mean and SD

Mean = the average. SD (standard deviation) = how spread out people are around the average. About two-thirds of people lie within 1 SD of the mean.

SHAI mean 12.3 ± SD 5.8 → most people scored between about 6.5 and 18
The average health-anxiety score was 12.3 out of 42.
3. Median and IQR

The median is the middle person. The IQR is the range of the middle half. We use them when scores are skewed (not a symmetrical bell shape).

SHAI median 12 (IQR 8–16): half of people scored 8 to 16
4. Normal distribution and why it matters

Many tests assume scores form a bell curve. The Shapiro–Wilk test checks this. Our scores were slightly skewed (p < 0.01), so we used non-parametric tests (they do not need the bell shape) and ordinary (multiple linear and logistic) regression.

Our scores were not perfectly normal, so we used tests that do not assume normality.
5. p-value

Imagine there is truly no link. The p-value is the chance of seeing a result as strong as ours just by luck. Small p (below 0.05) = "unlikely to be luck" = significant. It does not say how big or important the effect is.

r = 0.47, p < 0.001 → almost impossible to get this by chance if there were no link
p tells you if an effect exists, not how big it is. For size, look at r, β, OR.
6. 95% confidence interval (CI)

Our number is an estimate from 300 people. The CI is the range where the true value probably lies. Narrow CI = precise. If a CI for an odds ratio includes 1, the result is not significant.

r = 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.55 → the true correlation is probably between 0.38 and 0.55: always clearly above zero
7. Correlation r

How closely two scores move together: 0 = no link, 0.1 small, 0.3 moderate, 0.5 large, 1 = perfect. Positive = both go up together.

r = 0.47: people with more health anxiety tend to have more cyberchondria. r² = 22%: health anxiety explains about a fifth of the differences in cyberchondria.
Health anxiety and cyberchondria rise together: r = 0.47, a moderate link.
8. Pearson vs Spearman

Pearson r assumes a straight-line link; Spearman ρ uses ranks and does not need normal data. We report both; both were 0.47, so the result does not depend on the method.

9. Partial correlation

The same correlation after "removing" other variables (age, gender, background, chronic disease, trust, AI use). If it stays the same, those variables are not the explanation.

r = 0.47 → partial r = 0.48: unchanged
The link stayed the same after adjusting for everything else, so it is not explained by age, gender or background.
10. Comparing groups: Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis

To compare a score between groups without assuming normality: Mann–Whitney U for 2 groups, Kruskal–Wallis H for 3 or more.

Chronic condition: SHAI 14.2 vs 11.9 → Mann–Whitney p = 0.004
11. Chi-square (χ²)

To test if two categorical variables are related, e.g. "elevated health anxiety yes/no" and "uses social media yes/no". It compares the counts we saw with the counts we would expect if there were no link.

12. Many tests and chance findings

Like most graduation theses, we used the usual rule: p < 0.05 = significant. Because we ran many tests, a few “significant” results could be luck (with 100 tests at 0.05, about 5 would be). We did not adjust the p-values; instead we say so honestly in the limitations and we rely most on results that are strong (p < 0.001) and consistent across analyses.

We used p below 0.05; because we ran many tests, we mention in the limitations that some weaker results may be due to chance.
13. Linear regression, B and β

Regression predicts a score (CSS) from several factors at the same time, so each factor is "adjusted" for the others. B = how many CSS points change per 1 unit of the factor. β = the same on a common scale, so you can compare factors (bigger β = stronger).

SHAI: B = 0.68, β = 0.48. Someone with SHAI 20 vs 10 → about 6.8 CSS points higher.
Each extra point of health anxiety adds about 0.7 points of cyberchondria; it is by far the strongest factor.
14. R², ΔR² and hierarchical regression

R² = share of differences the model explains (0–1). In hierarchical regression we add groups of variables in steps and see how much each step adds (ΔR²).

Step 1 demographics R² 0.030 (3%) → step 2 digital use 0.133 (13%) → step 3 health anxiety 0.317 (32%). Health anxiety adds 18.4%.
Hierarchical order shows that health anxiety matters even after everything else is already in the model.
15. Checking the regression: VIF

If two predictors are almost the same thing, the regression cannot separate them (multicollinearity). SPSS gives the VIF for each predictor: below 5 is fine. Ours were all low.

16. Odds and odds ratios (logistic regression)

Logistic regression predicts a yes/no outcome (elevated health anxiety yes/no). It gives odds ratios (OR): OR 1 = no effect, above 1 = higher odds, below 1 = lower odds. Adjusted OR (AOR) = after accounting for the other factors.

Worked example from our data:
With elevated health anxiety: 52.6% had high cyberchondria → odds = 52.6/47.4 = 1.11
Without: 23.0% → odds = 23.0/77.0 = 0.30
Odds ratio = 1.11 / 0.30 ≈ 3.7

In the model: AOR 3.12 per 1 SD of cyberchondria (1 SD ≈ 8.4 CSS points) means about three times the odds for every 8 extra points.

AOR below 1 means protective. Age AOR 0.61: each older age group had lower odds.
17. AUC (ROC curve)

How well the logistic model separates people with and without elevated health anxiety. Pick one person with and one without: AUC is the chance the model gives the higher risk to the right one. 0.5 = coin toss, 0.7–0.8 acceptable, 0.8–0.9 good.

AUC 0.83: good
18. The Sobel test

Tests whether an indirect effect (a × b) is bigger than zero. You need a, its SE, b and its SE (from two regressions in SPSS): z = a×b / √(b²·SEa² + a²·SEb²). If |z| > 1.96, p < 0.05.

Model A: a = 0.46 (SE 0.05), b = 0.35 (SE 0.06) → Sobel z = 5.14, p < 0.001
19. Mediation (indirect effect)

Does part of the link from X to Y go through a middle variable M? Path a = X → M; path b = M → Y (adjusted for X); indirect = a × b; direct (c′) = what is left.

Model A: health anxiety → excessive searching → distress
a = 0.46, b = 0.35 → indirect = 0.46 × 0.35 ≈ 0.16
total = 0.46, direct = 0.30 → about 35% goes through excessive searching
Model C: health anxiety → distress → reassurance seeking: indirect 0.22, direct 0.01 → almost all goes through distress
Mediation in one survey is NOT proof of cause. The reverse order was also significant (0.15). Always say: compatible with a cycle, not proof.
20. Hosmer–Lemeshow test

Checks whether a logistic model fits the data: here we want a non-significant p (p > 0.05 = good fit).

Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.50 → good fit
21. Reliability: Cronbach α

Do the items of a scale measure the same thing? 0.7 acceptable, 0.8 good, 0.9 excellent.

SHAI α 0.81, CSS α 0.86: good
22. Fisher's exact test and Cramér's V

χ² needs enough people in each cell (expected count ≥ 5). When cells are small, Fisher's exact test gives the p-value instead. Cramér's V is the strength of a χ² association: 0.1 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large.

23. z-scores (standardised scores)

A z-score shows how far someone is from the average in SDs (0 = average, +1 = one SD above). We used them so odds ratios are “per 1 SD” and mediation coefficients are comparable. In SPSS: Descriptives → “Save standardized values”.

24. Where each test is in SPSS

Frequencies: Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Frequencies · Reliability: Analyze → Scale → Reliability Analysis · Correlation: Analyze → Correlate → Bivariate / Partial · Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis: Analyze → Nonparametric Tests → Legacy Dialogs · χ²: Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Crosstabs → Statistics · Linear regression: Analyze → Regression → Linear (Next for each block) · Logistic: Analyze → Regression → Binary Logistic · AUC: Analyze → Classify → ROC Curve. All of it is in our syntax file.

25. Sensitivity analysis

Re-doing the main analysis in another way (excluding doubtful responses, removing one item, another method) to see if the result changes. Ours did not change: r stayed 0.46–0.48.

9Questions and model answers

A. Questions on your part

Q: Why non-parametric tests?

Shapiro–Wilk showed the scores were not normally distributed; Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis do not assume normality.

Q: Explain r = 0.47 to a non-statistician.

People with more health anxiety clearly tend to have more cyberchondria; anxiety accounts for about a fifth (22%) of the differences.

Q: Why both Pearson and Spearman?

Pearson for the linear correlation, Spearman as a check that does not depend on normality; both were 0.47.

Q: What does partial r = 0.48 tell us?

The link is not explained by age, gender, background, chronic disease, trust or AI use: it survives adjustment (H2).

Q: Why hierarchical regression?

To see what health anxiety adds after demographics and digital use: 18.4% extra variance, by far the biggest block.

Q: What do B and β mean in Table 8?

B = 0.68: each SHAI point adds about 0.7 CSS points. β = 0.48: the standardised effect, used to compare predictors.

Q: What is an adjusted odds ratio of 3.12?

For each standard deviation higher cyberchondria, the odds of elevated health anxiety are about three times higher, holding other factors constant.

Q: Why is the reassurance-motive AOR so wide?

Only 57 events for 11 predictors (≈ 5 per variable), so estimates are imprecise and probably optimistic; we interpret them cautiously.

Q: Does the mediation prove a vicious cycle?

No. One time point, the reverse order fits too, and the facets come from one questionnaire. It is compatible with a cycle; only longitudinal data can show direction.

Q: Why does the AI effect disappear?

It depends on 32 people who named a chatbot without ticking it; they had the highest scores. Under the strict definition p = 0.21. Only 34 non-users made a small comparison group.

Q: Chronic illness was not significant in the logistic model. Why?

Dichotomising SHAI at 18 loses information and power. On the continuous score chronic illness is significant: p = 0.004 unadjusted and p = 0.029 adjusted (B +1.7).

Q: Why is high cyberchondria defined as > 33?

There is no validated clinical cut-off for the CSS-12, so we used the upper tertile of our sample, as is common.

Q: Why is the prevalence so dependent on the cut-off (Table 4)?

Because most scores cluster just below the thresholds; that is why we report several cut-offs and say “elevated”, not “disorder”.

Q: Which software did you use?

IBM SPSS Statistics [version]; the syntax file reproduces every table (say this only after the team has run it and checked the numbers).

Q: Why didn't you correct for multiple testing?

We used the standard p < 0.05, as most graduation theses do, and we say in the limitations that with many tests some weaker results may be chance. Our main results are p < 0.001.

Q: What is the AUC?

The area under the ROC curve: how well the model separates elevated from non-elevated anxiety. 0.83 is good.

Q: What does the Hosmer–Lemeshow test tell you?

Whether the logistic model fits the data. We want it non-significant: p = 0.50, so the fit is good.

Q: How did you test mediation?

Three linear regressions per model (paths a, b, c and c′) and the Sobel test for the indirect effect a × b.

Q: What does Sobel z = 5.14 mean?

The indirect effect is about five standard errors above zero, so p < 0.001: part of the link runs through excessive searching, statistically.

Q: Why Mann–Whitney and not the t-test?

Shapiro–Wilk showed the scores were not normal (p < 0.01), so we used non-parametric tests that compare ranks.

Why didn't you correct for multiple tests?We used the standard p < 0.05, like most graduation theses, and we say in the limitations that with many tests some weaker results may be chance. Our main results have p < 0.001.
What is regression for?To see which factors matter when we look at all of them together.
What is β?The strength of each factor on a common scale, so we can compare them. Health anxiety had the biggest, 0.48.
What is an odds ratio of 3?The odds are three times higher. Below 1 means lower odds.
What is AUC?How well our model tells anxious from non-anxious people apart: 0.83 is good.
What is mediation?Checking whether part of a link passes through a middle step, here searching or distress.
Does mediation prove a cycle?No. The reverse also fits, and it is one survey at one time point.
Why did the AI effect disappear?It depended on 32 people who named a chatbot but did not tick the box; only 34 non-users. With the strict definition it was not significant.
Why non-parametric tests?Our scores were not perfectly normal; these tests do not need that.
What is Cronbach's alpha?Whether the questions of a scale measure the same thing; ours were 0.81 and 0.86, good.
What is the Sobel test?A test of whether the indirect effect (a × b) is really above zero. Ours were z = 5.1 and 6.0, both p < 0.001.
What is a sensitivity analysis?Doing the analysis in other ways to see if the result changes. It did not.
Why both a table and a figure for some results?The figure shows the pattern; the table gives the exact numbers. We did both for the most important results.
Why a box plot for Figure 5?Cyberchondria scores are skewed, so the median and spread are more honest than a bar of means.
Why a forest plot for Figure 6?It shows many odds ratios and their CIs at once; anything crossing 1 is not significant.
Why is the item-by-item figure only on a slide, not in the thesis?It has 168 numbers: the thesis reports the key result in one sentence; the full figure is in the long version.
