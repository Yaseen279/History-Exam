Part A · Six ideas every result uses

Every number in Chapter 3 is one of these six things. Learn them once and every table becomes readable.

1. Two kinds of variable decide everything

Categorical
Groups or yes/no: gender, uses AI (yes/no), elevated health anxiety (yes/no). Described with n and %.
Continuous (a score)
A number on a scale: SHAI-14 (0–42), CSS-12 (12–60). Described with mean ± SD or median (IQR).

Which test you use depends only on what kind of variable you are comparing with what (Part B).

2. The p-value: “could this be luck?”

Imagine there is really no difference or no link. The p-value is the chance of getting a result at least as strong as ours just by luck. If p < 0.05 we call it significant: unlikely to be luck.

Example

Health anxiety and cyberchondria: r = 0.47, p < 0.001. If there were no link, a correlation this strong in 300 people would happen less than 1 time in 1000.

Trap

p tells you whether an effect exists, not how big or important it is. With 300 people even small effects can be significant. For size, look at r, B, β or the odds ratio.

3. The 95% confidence interval: “how sure are we?”

Our numbers come from 300 people, not everyone. The 95% CI is the range where the true value probably lies. Narrow = precise.

Two rules you will use all the time

Odds ratio: if the CI includes 1 → not significant. Example: chronic condition AOR 1.51 (0.69–3.30) includes 1 → not significant.

Difference or coefficient (B): if the CI includes 0 → not significant. Example: Female B −0.05 (−1.82 to 1.72) includes 0 → not significant.

5. Unadjusted versus adjusted

Unadjusted (bivariate, Tables 5–7): one variable at a time. Adjusted (multivariable, Tables 8–10): many variables in one model, so each result is “holding the others constant”. A factor that is significant alone can disappear after adjustment if another factor explains it, and the reverse can happen too (you will see both in Table 8).

6. Normal or not normal

Many tests (t-test, ANOVA) assume scores form a bell curve. The Shapiro–Wilk test checks this. Ours were not normal (SHAI p < 0.001; CSS p = 0.008), so groups were compared with non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis), which compare ranks instead of means. Correlation and regression were kept because n = 300 is large, Spearman gave the same answer as Pearson, and the regression residuals were close to normal (checked with a histogram and P-P plot).

If they ask “why no ANOVA or t-test?”

“Shapiro–Wilk showed our scores were not normally distributed and some groups were small, so we used Kruskal–Wallis, the non-parametric version of one-way ANOVA, and Mann–Whitney instead of the t-test. We checked: ANOVA and t-tests give the same conclusions.”

If they ask

QuestionDo people with more health anxiety have more cyberchondria (H1), and does it hold after adjustment (H2)?

Walk-through

  1. CSS total: r = 0.47 (0.38–0.55). The whole CI is well above 0 → a real, moderate-to-large link.
  2. r² = 0.47 × 0.47 = 0.22 → health anxiety accounts for about 22% of the differences in cyberchondria.
  3. Spearman ρ = 0.47 → identical: the result does not depend on the method.
  4. Partial r = 0.48 after removing age, gender, health background, chronic disease, trust and AI use → unchanged, so those are not the explanation (H2).
  5. By dimension: excessiveness 0.46 and distress 0.45 are strongest; compulsion 0.25 and reassurance 0.24 weakest (as H1 predicted).
Reading the line: CSS = 20.5 + 0.67 × SHAI SHAI 10 → 20.5 + 6.7 = 27.2 SHAI 20 → 20.5 + 13.4 = 33.9 (about 7 CSS points higher for 10 SHAI points)
Say it

This is our main result. Health anxiety and cyberchondria rise together, r = 0.47, and the link is the same after adjusting for everything else, 0.48. It is strongest for excessive searching and for distress after searching. Hypotheses 1 and 2 are supported.

Trap

Say “associated with” or “linked to”. Never “health anxiety causes cyberchondria”: one survey at one time cannot show direction.

If they ask

Why is your r (0.47) lower than the meta-analyses (0.62)?

Our health anxiety scores were low and narrowly spread, our participants were confident internet users, and we used the short 12-item scale; all of these weaken correlations.

Pearson or Spearman, which is right?

Both: Pearson is the standard for two scores, Spearman checks it without assuming normality, and they agree (0.47).

If they ask

Why is high cyberchondria “> 33”?

The CSS-12 has no validated cut-off, so we used the upper third of our sample.

Why Fisher sometimes?

χ² is unreliable when expected counts in a cell are below 5; Fisher's exact test is used then.

Walk-through

  1. Health anxiety: B = 0.68 (0.53–0.84), β = 0.48, p < 0.001 → each extra SHAI point adds 0.68 CSS points; 10 points more anxiety ≈ 7 points more cyberchondria. The largest β by far.

  3. Age 30–39 (B 6.27, p = 0.001) and ≥ 40 (B 3.98, p = 0.041) higher than 20–29. Why, when Table 6 showed no age difference in CSS (p = 0.288)? Say it

Who you are explains only 3% of cyberchondria; digital habits add 10%, and health anxiety adds another 18%, to 32% in total. Health anxiety is by far the strongest predictor, beta 0.48. The AI effect disappears when only people who ticked AI are counted.

If they ask

What is the difference between B and β?

B is in real units (CSS points per SHAI point); β puts every predictor on the same scale so their strength can be compared.

Why hierarchical?

To show what health anxiety adds after demographics and digital use are already in the model: 18.4%, the biggest block.

Trap

81% correct sounds impressive, but 243 of 300 (81%) did not have elevated anxiety, so a “model” that always guessed “no” would also be 81% correct. That is why the AUC (0.83) is the number to quote for how well the model works.

Say it

Three factors stand out: higher cyberchondria triples the odds of elevated health anxiety, searching for reassurance nearly quadruples them, and older age is protective. The model fits well and its AUC is 0.83.

If they ask

Why are the odds ratios so large and wide?

Only 57 people had elevated anxiety for 11 predictors, about 5 per variable, so the estimates are imprecise and may be overestimated. We say so in the limitations.

Is the adjusted SHAI difference significant? B = 1.23, 95% CI −0.15 to 2.60 → the interval includes 0 → not significant (p = 0.081)
Unadjusted vs adjusted: the key lesson of this table

A difference between two groups can be caused by a third variable that differs between them (here, age). Adjustment removes that third variable. If the difference disappears, the group itself was not the cause.

Say it

Before adjustment, medical students had higher health anxiety, 13.3 against 11.6, but after adjusting for age and other factors the difference was no longer significant, because medical students are younger. Their cyberchondria was the same as everyone else's. Hypothesis 2 was not supported.

Trap

Do not say “medical students are more anxious”. Say “they scored higher only before adjustment; the difference reflected their younger age”.

If they ask

Is “medical students' disease” real, then?

Not in our sample: knowing more about disease did not make students search more anxiously, but it did not protect them either. A Palestinian study with the same SHAI cut-off agrees; an Egyptian study found high cyberchondria in medical students but had no comparison group.

Why adjust for these four variables?

Age, gender, chronic disease and language can all affect health anxiety and differ between medical students and the others, so they could create a false difference.

Medical students were 44% of the sample. Does that bias the main result?

Being a medical student did not change health anxiety or cyberchondria after adjustment, so the high share of medical students is unlikely to explain the main association.
