1 · The study in full

Title

Health Anxiety, AI Chatbot Use and Cyberchondria Among Adults in Baghdad: An Online Cross-Sectional Survey

Abstract (as in the thesis)

Background: Adults increasingly consult search engines and, more recently, artificial-intelligence (AI) chatbots before seeing a doctor. Health anxiety and cyberchondria (excessive, distressing online health searching) are thought to reinforce each other, but evidence from Iraq, and on AI chatbots, is scarce.

Objectives: To measure the association between health anxiety and cyberchondria among adults in Baghdad, and to describe online and AI health-information seeking and pre-consultation behaviour.

Methods: Cross-sectional online survey of 300 adults in Baghdad (July–September 2026) using the Short Health Anxiety Inventory (SHAI-14) and the Cyberchondria Severity Scale (CSS-12). Analyses included measurement-invariance testing across Arabic and English responders, FDR-corrected group comparisons, robust regression and bootstrapped mediation.

Results: Participants were mostly young (62.3% aged 20–29) and 63.3% had a health-related background; the CSS-12 was invariant across Arabic and English responders. AI chatbots were ticked by 78.0% (88.7% including respondents who named a chatbot), against 56.3% for search engines. Health anxiety correlated with cyberchondria (r = 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.55) and remained its strongest predictor after adjustment (β = 0.48), most strongly through excessive searching and post-search distress. Elevated health anxiety (SHAI-14 ≥ 18, a screening threshold rather than a diagnosis) was found in 19.0%; only 0.7% reached the stricter low-prevalence cut-off of 29. After searching, 34.7% delayed or skipped care because they felt reassured and 31.7% never told their doctor, citing fear of judgement; higher cyberchondria was associated with more disclosure. The association between AI use and cyberchondria was not robust to how AI use was defined.

Conclusion: In this convenience sample of mostly young, health-oriented adults, health anxiety and cyberchondria were linked, and AI chatbots were a major channel for online health information, without reliable evidence that their use adds to cyberchondria beyond health anxiety. The cross-sectional design cannot establish direction. Clinicians should ask about online and AI searches without judgement.

Keywords: cyberchondria; health anxiety; artificial intelligence; chatbots; online health information seeking; Iraq.

Why the study was done (Rationale, 1.7)

This study addresses three gaps. First, it provides, to our knowledge, the first estimate of the association between health anxiety and cyberchondria (using the CSS-12) in a Baghdad-based sample. Second, it examines AI chatbots as a distinct, and now dominant, source of online health information, rather than treating "the Internet" as one entity. Third, it links these psychological constructs to concrete pre-consultation behaviours, namely delaying care, self-medication, urgent visits, multiple consultations, disclosure of searches and trust in physicians, that matter directly to clinicians.

Aim, objectives and hypotheses (1.8, word for word)

Aim: To study the relationship between health anxiety and cyberchondria and their correlates among adults in Baghdad who search for health information online, with particular attention to AI chatbots and pre-consultation behaviour.

Specific objectives:

1. To describe online and AI-based health-information seeking and pre-consultation behaviour among adults in Baghdad.

2. To estimate levels of health anxiety and cyberchondria and the strength of their association, overall and by cyberchondria dimension, independently of socio-demographic and digital-use factors (H1, H2); correlates of each construct were examined as secondary analyses.

3. To examine statistical pathways consistent with a mutually reinforcing cycle, and factors that might modify the association (H3).

4. To examine how health anxiety and cyberchondria relate to pre-consultation behaviour, disclosure of searches to physicians and trust in physicians (H4).

Hypotheses: (H1) Health anxiety is positively associated with cyberchondria, most strongly with its distress and excessiveness dimensions. (H2) The association is independent of socio-demographic and digital-use factors. (H3) Excessive searching statistically mediates the link between health anxiety and post-search distress. (H4) Higher cyberchondria is associated with care-seeking behaviours (urgent visits, multiple consultations) and with a more strained doctor–patient relationship. H1 and H2 were the primary, confirmatory hypotheses and H3 and H4 secondary; all other analyses, including the AI-use comparisons, item-level, moderation and sensitivity analyses, were exploratory.

Methods (2.1–2.5, word for word; 2.6 is in Section 7)

This was an analytical cross-sectional study reported in line with the STROBE statement for observational studies [37] and the CHERRIES checklist for web-based surveys [38]. Data were collected online in Baghdad, Iraq, between 17 July 2026 and 11 September 2026.

2.2 Participants and sampling

A non-probability (convenience and snowball) sampling approach was used. The survey link was distributed through online channels (social media and messaging groups) whose members were based in Baghdad. Inclusion criteria were: age 18 years or older, ability to read Arabic or English, and provision of informed consent. Every completed submission was retained for analysis. No duplicate response patterns were found. [Authors to state whether Google Forms limited submissions to one per account.] Because the link spread by snowball sampling, the number of people who saw the invitation is unknown, so view and participation rates could not be computed. Three responses had been annotated by the team during data collection [authors to state the reason for each annotation]; they were kept, and their exclusion was tested in a sensitivity analysis. The resulting sample was predominantly young and from health-related fields (Section 3.1).

2.3 Sample size

The study was powered for its primary objective, the correlation between health anxiety and cyberchondria. Using Fisher's z approximation with two-sided α = 0.05 and 80% power, a minimum of 194 participants is required to detect a small-to-moderate correlation (r = 0.20), and 85 to detect r = 0.30 [39]. Meta-analytic estimates of this association are far larger (r ≈ 0.62) [18,19], so these targets are conservative. For descriptive proportions, 300 participants give a margin of error of ±5.7% at 95% confidence (worst case, p = 0.5), compared with ±5% for the 385 participants of a Cochran-based calculation. The logistic model for elevated health anxiety had 57 events for 11 predictors (≈5 events per variable), which is acknowledged as a limitation of that model.

2.4 Data collection instrument

A bilingual (Arabic–English) self-administered questionnaire was built in Google Forms. All items were required, except the free-text description of chronic disease and the name of the AI chatbot used, which were conditional. The questionnaire had five parts:

1. Consent and socio-demographic data: confirmation of age ≥18 and consent; age group; gender; highest education; nature of study or work; diagnosed chronic medical condition (and its type).

2. Digital health information seeking: tools used (search engines, AI chatbots, dedicated medical platforms, social media; multiple answers); main AI chatbot; motivation for searching (multiple answers); and trust in medical information generated by online tools/AI (5-point scale).

3. Health anxiety: the 14-item Short Health Anxiety Inventory (SHAI-14) [40]. Each item offers four statements scored 0–3, giving a total of 0–42; higher scores indicate greater health anxiety. The SHAI has good reliability and validity in clinical and non-clinical samples, including recent validations in Spain, Sweden and Iran [41–43], and an Arabic version has been validated in Saudi Arabia [44].

4. Cyberchondria: the CSS-12 [14], with items rated on a 5-point frequency scale (1 = never to 5 = always), giving a total of 12–60 and four subscales (3–15 each): Excessiveness (items 1–3), Compulsion (4–6), Distress (7–9) and Reassurance seeking (10–12).

5. Pre-consultation behaviour and relationship with physicians: behavioural responses after searching (multiple answers), whether search or AI results are disclosed to the physician, the perceived effect of digital information seeking on the relationship with and trust in physicians, and the language mainly relied upon while answering.

Each item was presented in English and Arabic. The research team produced the Arabic wording; its equivalence was examined empirically by comparing the psychometric behaviour of the scales between participants who relied mainly on Arabic and those who relied mainly on English (Section 2.6). For one SHAI item (item 2, noticing aches and pains) the four statements were displayed in a different order from the original scale ("as much as most people" before "less than most people"); all other SHAI and CSS items appeared in their original order. Items were scored according to the meaning of the chosen statement, following the original scoring key. The Arabic wording was produced by the research team rather than through formal cross-cultural adaptation [45], and the published Arabic versions of the CSS-12 and SHAI [15,44,46] were not used. As in the original scale, CSS item 3 refers to entering symptoms into a search engine.

2.5 Variables and operational definitions

Elevated health anxiety: SHAI-14 ≥ 18, the optimal cut-off for distinguishing people with health anxiety from healthy controls in a Swedish diagnostic-accuracy study [42]. Because the same study recommends higher cut-offs in psychiatric (22) and low-prevalence (29) settings, the term "elevated" rather than "clinically relevant" is used, it is not a diagnosis, and prevalence at 15, 22 and 29 is also reported

High cyberchondria: CSS-12 total in the upper tertile of the sample (> 33), since the CSS-12 has no validated clinical cut-off.

AI-chatbot user: ticked "AI chatbots" as a tool or named an AI chatbot in the follow-up question. Thirty-two respondents named a chatbot without ticking the tool option; they were classified as users (the follow-up question was optional and specifically addressed to chatbot users). Because this decision affects the results, AI use is reported both ways (ticked only, and ticked or named), and all analyses involving AI use were repeated with the stricter definition.

Health-related background: medical students, students in other health fields and healthcare workers.

Occupation: free-text "Other" answers were recoded into the closest category (e.g. secondary-school students and school leavers as non-health students; housewives, engineers and business owners as non-health employed/other). Age groups 40–49, 50–59 and 60+ were merged (≥40) for inferential analysis because of small cell sizes.

Conclusions (5.1, word for word)

1. AI chatbots, mainly ChatGPT, have become a leading source of health information before consulting a doctor among digitally active adults in Baghdad: 78.0% ticked them (88.7% including those who named a chatbot), more than used search engines.

2. About one in five participants (19%) had elevated health anxiety (SHAI ≥ 18, a screening threshold rather than a diagnosis; 0.7% at the stricter cut-off of 29), and younger people and those searching for instant reassurance were most affected.

3. Health anxiety and cyberchondria are moderately and robustly associated (r = 0.47); health anxiety is the strongest independent predictor of cyberchondria, especially of excessive searching and post-search distress.

4. The pattern of associations is compatible with a reinforcing cycle (health anxiety, excessive searching, distress, reassurance-seeking consultations), but the cross-sectional decomposition, based on subscales of one instrument, cannot establish direction.

5. There is no reliable evidence that AI-chatbot use is associated with cyberchondria independently of health anxiety: the association depended on how inconsistent answers about AI use were classified, and AI use did not change the strength of the health anxiety–cyberchondria link.

6. Online searching shapes the consultation: about one-third of participants delay care because they feel reassured, about one-third never disclose their searches to their doctor for fear of judgement, and about one-third say searching has complicated or reduced their trust in physicians. Higher cyberchondria was robustly linked with more disclosure; links with urgent care-seeking, concealment by health-anxious participants and a strained relationship were exploratory.

Recommendations (5.2, word for word)

For clinicians and medical education:

Routinely and non-judgementally ask patients what they have searched for or asked an AI chatbot, to bring hidden information into the consultation and reduce concealment.

Recognise repeated searching, post-search distress and repeated reassurance seeking as possible markers of health anxiety, and screen with a brief tool such as the SHAI where appropriate; offer cognitive-behavioural strategies rather than repeated reassurance or investigations.

Include digital health communication, AI literacy and the management of health anxiety in undergraduate medical curricula, and support medical students who may themselves be vulnerable.

For the Ministry of Health and public-health bodies:

May consider campaigns on digital and AI health literacy that explain both the uses and the limits of online information, especially false reassurance that leads to delayed care.

May consider providing or endorsing reliable Arabic-language health information sources and triage services so that people searching for "whether I need a doctor" are directed to safe advice.

For developers of AI tools:

May consider designing health responses that recognise signs of health anxiety (e.g. repeated questions about the same symptom), avoid escalating rare diagnoses without context, and encourage appropriate professional consultation, in Arabic as well as English.

For future research:

Develop and test interventions: ClinicalTrials.gov lists only nine registered studies on cyberchondria (October 2026), none in Iraq and six of them observational and three interventional [60], so trials of brief digital health-literacy or cognitive-behavioural interventions in Arabic are a clear gap.

Conduct longitudinal or experience-sampling studies to test whether health anxiety and cyberchondria reinforce each other over time, and experimental studies comparing AI chatbot and search-engine answers on post-search anxiety.

Use probability sampling of the general Baghdad population, including older and less-educated adults, and use formally adapted Arabic instruments (the published CSS-12-Ar and Arabic SHAI, or a new forward–backward adaptation with measurement-invariance testing), with CSS item 3 worded to cover both search engines and AI chatbots.

Link self-reported behaviours to objective healthcare utilisation and clinical outcomes, especially delayed diagnosis following false reassurance.

Strengths and limitations (4.8, word for word)

Strengths of this study include the use of two established instruments with good reliability and confirmed structure; a bilingual questionnaire with formal tests of measurement invariance across languages; the separate assessment of AI chatbots; complete data; correction for multiple testing; robust regression, bootstrap inference and several sensitivity analyses; and the linking of psychological measures to concrete behaviours relevant to clinical practice.

The limitations are substantial and should be weighed carefully:

Design and causality: the cross-sectional design cannot establish temporal order; mediation results describe statistical pathways, not causal mechanisms, and may be biased relative to longitudinal effects.

Sampling and generalisability: an online convenience/snowball sample is subject to self-selection. The sample was young, highly educated and over-represented medical students (44.0%) and people with health backgrounds (63.3%), so prevalence estimates and the frequency of AI use cannot be generalised to the adult population of Baghdad. Residence in Baghdad was assured by the distribution channels rather than verified by a question, and non-Internet users, older adults and less-educated people are under-represented by design.

Measurement: the Arabic wording was produced by the research team without a formal forward–backward translation and cognitive pre-testing; formal tests supported full (scalar) invariance of the CSS-12 and metric invariance of the SHAI-14 across language groups, with no item showing DIF after correction, but SHAI-14 intercepts were not fully equivalent, the language groups were self-selected rather than randomised, and statistical invariance does not replace formal cross-cultural adaptation with cognitive pre-testing. Published Arabic versions of both scales exist [15,44,46] but were not used, so comparability with Arabic studies that used them is uncertain. On the form, the response options of one SHAI item (item 2) were displayed out of their original order; scoring followed the original key, and dropping the item did not change the main result, but the order may still have influenced answers. CSS item 3 refers only to search engines and may under-capture repeated querying of AI chatbots. "Language relied on" was self-selected, not randomised. The SHAI-14 one-factor model fitted poorly under maximum-likelihood estimation. The SHAI cut-off was derived in Sweden, and the CSS-12 has no validated clinical cut-off, so the "high cyberchondria" group is sample-relative.

Single-item and self-designed measures: trust, motivations, behaviours, disclosure and relationship were assessed with single, non-validated items, some allowing multiple answers. The questionnaire asked for "most frequent" behaviours but allowed several answers; the trust item combined online tools and AI, so it cannot separate trust in search results from trust in chatbots; the disclosure option combined frequency with a reason (fear of judgement); and "somewhat complicated" is not clearly a negative effect on the relationship.

Self-report bias: all measures were self-reported and may be affected by recall and social desirability bias; no clinical data or diagnoses were available, and chronic disease was self-reported.

Multiple testing and power: despite FDR correction, the large number of exploratory tests means that some isolated associations may be false positives; several behavioural associations were nominally significant only. Some subgroups (non-AI users, n = 34; healthcare workers, n = 15; older age groups) were small, and the logistic model for clinical health anxiety had about five events per predictor.

Classification of AI use: 32 respondents named an AI chatbot without ticking the AI option. They were classed as users, and the independent association between AI use and cyberchondria depended on this decision, so it should be regarded as tentative.

Data quality: an online survey cannot exclude careless responding entirely; nine straight-line responders were identified, although their exclusion did not change the results.

4 · Statistics from zero (with our numbers)

Each lesson: the idea in plain words, our example, and what to say. Read them in order; each one builds on the one before.

1. Variables: two kinds
  • Categorical = groups (gender, uses AI yes/no, tool profile). We describe them with n and %.
  • Continuous = scores on a scale (SHAI 0–42, CSS 12–60). We describe them with mean, SD, median.

This one fact decides almost every choice of test and chart in the thesis.

2. Mean and SD

Mean = the average. SD (standard deviation) = how spread out people are around the average. About two-thirds of people lie within 1 SD of the mean.

SHAI mean 12.3 ± SD 5.8 → most people scored between about 6.5 and 18
The average health-anxiety score was 12.3 out of 42.
3. Median and IQR

The median is the middle person. The IQR is the range of the middle half. We use them when scores are skewed (not a symmetrical bell shape).

SHAI median 12 (IQR 8–16): half of people scored 8 to 16
4. Normal distribution and why it matters

Many tests assume scores form a bell curve. The Shapiro–Wilk test checks this. Our scores were slightly skewed (p < 0.01), so we used non-parametric tests (they do not need the bell shape) and robust regression.

Our scores were not perfectly normal, so we used tests that do not assume normality.
5. p-value

Imagine there is truly no link. The p-value is the chance of seeing a result as strong as ours just by luck. Small p (below 0.05) = "unlikely to be luck" = significant. It does not say how big or important the effect is.

r = 0.47, p < 0.001 → almost impossible to get this by chance if there were no link
p tells you if an effect exists, not how big it is. For size, look at r, β, OR.
6. 95% confidence interval (CI)

Our number is an estimate from 300 people. The CI is the range where the true value probably lies. Narrow CI = precise. If a CI for an odds ratio includes 1, the result is not significant.

r = 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.55 → the true correlation is probably between 0.38 and 0.55: always clearly above zero
7. Correlation r

How closely two scores move together: 0 = no link, 0.1 small, 0.3 moderate, 0.5 large, 1 = perfect. Positive = both go up together.

r = 0.47: people with more health anxiety tend to have more cyberchondria. r² = 22%: health anxiety explains about a fifth of the differences in cyberchondria.
Health anxiety and cyberchondria rise together: r = 0.47, a moderate link.
8. Pearson vs Spearman

Pearson r assumes a straight-line link; Spearman ρ uses ranks and does not need normal data. We report both; both were 0.47, so the result does not depend on the method.

9. Partial correlation

The same correlation after "removing" other variables (age, gender, background, chronic disease, trust, AI use). If it stays the same, those variables are not the explanation.

r = 0.47 → partial r = 0.48: unchanged
The link stayed the same after adjusting for everything else, so it is not explained by age, gender or background.
10. Comparing groups: Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis

To compare a score between groups without assuming normality: Mann–Whitney U for 2 groups, Kruskal–Wallis H for 3 or more.

Chronic condition: SHAI 14.2 vs 11.9 → Mann–Whitney p = 0.004
11. Chi-square (χ²)

To test if two categorical variables are related, e.g. "elevated health anxiety yes/no" and "uses social media yes/no". It compares the counts we saw with the counts we would expect if there were no link.

12. Many tests → FDR correction (q-value)

If you run 162 tests at p < 0.05, about 8 will be "significant" by pure luck (162 × 0.05 ≈ 8). The Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction adjusts p-values into q-values. q < 0.05 = robust; p < 0.05 but q ≥ 0.05 = exploratory (could be luck).

Never disclosing: CSS q = 0.012 (robust) · SHAI q = 0.098 (exploratory)
Because we ran many tests, we corrected for multiple testing; results that did not survive are called exploratory.
13. Linear regression, B and β

Regression predicts a score (CSS) from several factors at the same time, so each factor is "adjusted" for the others. B = how many CSS points change per 1 unit of the factor. β = the same on a common scale, so you can compare factors (bigger β = stronger).

SHAI: B = 0.68, β = 0.48. Someone with SHAI 20 vs 10 → about 6.8 CSS points higher.
Each extra point of health anxiety adds about 0.7 points of cyberchondria; it is by far the strongest factor.
14. R², ΔR² and hierarchical regression

R² = share of differences the model explains (0–1). In hierarchical regression we add groups of variables in steps and see how much each step adds (ΔR²).

Step 1 demographics R² 0.030 (3%) → step 2 digital use 0.133 (13%) → step 3 health anxiety 0.317 (32%). Health anxiety adds 18.4%.
Hierarchical order shows that health anxiety matters even after everything else is already in the model.
15. Robust standard errors (HC3)

Regression assumes the "errors" are equally spread. Ours were not (Breusch–Pagan p = 0.004). HC3 robust standard errors fix the p-values and CIs so they are still valid. You only need: "we used robust standard errors because the residuals were uneven".

16. Odds and odds ratios (logistic regression)

Logistic regression predicts a yes/no outcome (elevated health anxiety yes/no). It gives odds ratios (OR): OR 1 = no effect, above 1 = higher odds, below 1 = lower odds. Adjusted OR (AOR) = after accounting for the other factors.

Worked example from our data:
With elevated health anxiety: 52.6% had high cyberchondria → odds = 52.6/47.4 = 1.11
Without: 23.0% → odds = 23.0/77.0 = 0.30
Odds ratio = 1.11 / 0.30 ≈ 3.7

In the model: AOR 3.12 per 1 SD of cyberchondria (1 SD ≈ 8.4 CSS points) means about three times the odds for every 8 extra points.

AOR below 1 means protective. Age AOR 0.61: each older age group had lower odds.
17. AUC

How well the logistic model separates people with and without elevated health anxiety. Pick one person with and one without: AUC is the chance the model gives the higher risk to the right one. 0.5 = coin toss, 1 = perfect.

AUC 0.83, and 0.79 after correcting for optimism (bootstrap): good
A model always looks better on the data it was built on, so we corrected the AUC with 500 bootstrap repeats.
18. Bootstrap

A computer trick: draw 5000 new samples of 300 people from our own data (with replacement), redo the analysis each time, and see how much the result varies. That gives a CI without assuming normality. We used it for mediation and to check the regression coefficient.

19. Mediation (indirect effect)

Does part of the link from X to Y go through a middle variable M? Path a = X → M; path b = M → Y (adjusted for X); indirect = a × b; direct (c′) = what is left.

Model A: health anxiety → excessive searching → distress
a = 0.46, b = 0.35 → indirect = 0.46 × 0.35 ≈ 0.16
total = 0.46, direct = 0.30 → about 35% goes through excessive searching
Model C: health anxiety → distress → reassurance seeking: indirect 0.22, direct 0.01 → almost all goes through distress
Mediation in one survey is NOT proof of cause. The reverse order was also significant (0.15). Always say: compatible with a cycle, not proof.
20. Moderation (interaction)

Does a third variable change the strength of the link? E.g. is the SHAI–CSS link stronger in AI users? We tested 7 moderators; none were significant after FDR. So the link is similar in everyone.

21. Reliability: Cronbach α and McDonald ω

Do the items of a scale measure the same thing? 0.7 acceptable, 0.8 good, 0.9 excellent.

SHAI α 0.81, CSS α 0.86: good
22. CFA and fit indices

Confirmatory factor analysis checks whether the items group into the factors the scale claims (CSS-12 = four facets). Fit indices: CFI near 0.95 or higher = good; RMSEA and SRMR 0.08 or lower = acceptable.

CSS-12 four factors: CFI 0.95, RMSEA 0.070, SRMR 0.053 → acceptable · SHAI one factor: CFI 0.83 → poor (it has two factors)
23. Measurement invariance

Before comparing Arabic and English answers, we must check the questionnaire works the same in both, like checking two rulers measure the same centimetre. Three steps: configural (same shape), metric (items equally strong), scalar (same starting points). If fit does not get worse at a step (CFI drop ≤ 0.01), that step passes.

CSS-12 passed all three. SHAI-14 passed the first two.

24. DIF

Differential item functioning: does a single question behave differently in Arabic vs English for people with the same total score? None did after FDR correction.

25. Sensitivity analysis

Re-doing the main analysis in another way (excluding doubtful responses, removing one item, another method) to see if the result changes. Ours did not change: r stayed 0.46–0.48.

7 · Statistical analysis (Section 2.6, word for word)

You are the team's statistics person. Read this until you can explain each method in one sentence (Section 4 gives the plain-language version of each).

Analyses were carried out in Python 3 (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels, pingouin, factor_analyzer and semopy). The analysis code is fully scripted and reproducible. All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05.

Descriptive statistics: frequencies and percentages for categorical variables; mean ± SD and median (IQR) for scores. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, skewness and kurtosis.

Psychometrics: internal consistency by Cronbach's α (with 95% CI) and McDonald's ω [47]; corrected item–rest correlations; sampling adequacy (KMO, Bartlett's test); and confirmatory factor analysis by maximum likelihood (ML), with fit judged by CFI, TLI, RMSEA and SRMR against conventional criteria (CFI/TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA ≤ 0.06–0.08, SRMR ≤ 0.08), recognising that fixed cut-offs do not transfer exactly to every model [48]. Diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS) estimates on Pearson covariances are reported for comparison only, because their fit indices are not comparable with these cut-offs. Equivalence of the Arabic and English responders was tested by comparing α values with Feldt's test, factor-loading similarity with Tucker's congruence coefficient [49] (an index of approximate structural similarity) and adjusted mean differences. Formal measurement invariance was then tested by multi-group CFA (ML with a mean structure), fitting configural, metric (equal loadings) and scalar (equal loadings and intercepts) models in sequence; each step was judged by the χ² difference test and by a change in CFI of no more than −0.01 and in RMSEA of no more than 0.015 [50]. Differential item functioning (DIF) was examined for every item by ordinal logistic regression of the item on the scale total, language and their interaction, a standard approach for ordinal items [51], with FDR correction across the 26 items.

Correlation analysis: Pearson and Spearman correlations (bootstrapped 95% CIs) and partial correlations adjusted for covariates. Differences between dependent correlations (health anxiety with each CSS subscale) were tested with Williams' T2 test for dependent correlations. Item-level correlations were computed for every SHAI item with the CSS total and every CSS item with the SHAI total.

Group comparisons for every variable: Mann–Whitney U (rank-biserial r as effect size) or Kruskal–Wallis H (ε² as effect size) for SHAI, CSS and each CSS subscale, with Welch's t-test/ANOVA as parametric confirmation and pairwise Mann–Whitney post-hoc comparisons with Holm adjustment. Associations of all categorical variables with elevated health anxiety and high cyberchondria were tested with Pearson χ² or Fisher's exact test (Monte-Carlo exact p when expected counts were small), with Cramér's V. Pairwise associations among all categorical variables were also screened.

Multiple testing: because many associations were tested, p-values within each family of tests were adjusted with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure (q-values) [52]. The families were: the 162 group comparisons; the chi-square tests; the pairwise associations among categorical variables; the 40 coefficients of the behaviour models; the moderation terms; and the 168 item-level correlations. Unadjusted p-values are reported, and associations that do not survive correction are labelled as exploratory.

Multivariable models: hierarchical OLS regression of CSS on socio-demographics (block 1), digital-use variables (block 2) and SHAI (block 3), with HC3 robust standard errors, standardised coefficients and VIF checks; the reverse model (SHAI on CSS); models for each CSS subscale; quantile regression (25th–90th percentiles) and a quadratic term to examine non-linearity. Logistic regression identified predictors of elevated health anxiety (AORs; apparent AUC and AUC corrected for optimism by 500 bootstrap refits) and of each pre-consultation behaviour; ordinal logistic regression was used for the ordered disclosure and relationship outcomes.

Mediation and moderation: as an exploratory decomposition of the association into CSS facets, mediation models with bootstrap confidence intervals (5000 resamples) estimated how much of the link between health anxiety and post-search distress ran through excessive searching, and related pathways, including a reverse-direction model and a serial model [53]. Moderation of the SHAI–CSS association by trust, AI use, health background, gender, chronic disease, language and age was tested with interaction terms and simple slopes.

Sensitivity analyses: exclusion of annotated responses; exclusion of straight-line responders (identical answers to all items of a scale); alternative SHAI cut-offs; bootstrap of the main regression coefficient; and comparison of early versus late responders as a proxy for non-response bias.

9 · Questions and model answers

A. Questions on your part

Q: Why non-parametric tests?

Shapiro–Wilk showed the scores were not normally distributed; Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis do not assume normality. Welch tests confirmed the same conclusions.

Q: What is FDR and why did you use it?

With 162 comparisons, about 8 would be “significant” by chance at p < 0.05. Benjamini–Hochberg controls the proportion of false discoveries; results with q ≥ 0.05 are labelled exploratory.

Q: Explain r = 0.47 to a non-statistician.

People with more health anxiety clearly tend to have more cyberchondria; anxiety accounts for about a fifth (22%) of the differences.

Q: Why both Pearson and Spearman?

Pearson for the linear correlation, Spearman as a check that does not depend on normality; both were 0.47.

Q: What does partial r = 0.48 tell us?

The link is not explained by age, gender, background, chronic disease, trust or AI use: it survives adjustment (H2).

Q: Why hierarchical regression?

To see what health anxiety adds after demographics and digital use: 18.4% extra variance, by far the biggest block.

Q: What do B and β mean in Table 9?

B = 0.68: each SHAI point adds about 0.7 CSS points. β = 0.48: the standardised effect, used to compare predictors.

Q: Why robust (HC3) standard errors?

The residuals were heteroskedastic (Breusch–Pagan p = 0.004); HC3 gives valid standard errors anyway.

Q: What is an adjusted odds ratio of 3.12?

For each standard deviation higher cyberchondria, the odds of elevated health anxiety are about three times higher, holding other factors constant.

Q: Why is the reassurance-motive AOR so wide?

Only 57 events for 11 predictors (≈ 5 per variable), so estimates are imprecise and probably optimistic; we label them exploratory.

Q: What is the AUC and why corrected?

How well the model separates elevated from non-elevated anxiety. Models look better on their own data, so 500 bootstrap refits corrected 0.83 to 0.79.

Q: Does the mediation prove a vicious cycle?

No. One time point, the reverse order fits too, and the facets come from one questionnaire. It is compatible with a cycle; only longitudinal data can show direction.

Q: Why does the AI effect disappear?

It depends on 32 people who named a chatbot without ticking it; they had the highest scores. Under the strict definition p = 0.31. Only 34 non-users made a small comparison group.

Q: Chronic illness was not significant in the logistic model. Why?

Dichotomising SHAI at 18 loses information and power. On the continuous score chronic illness is significant: p = 0.004 unadjusted and p = 0.029 adjusted (B +1.7).

Q: What do CFI, RMSEA and SRMR mean?

Fit indices for CFA. CSS-12 four factors: CFI 0.95, RMSEA 0.070, SRMR 0.053: acceptable. SHAI one-factor fitted poorly.

Q: Why does the SHAI fit poorly?

It is not strictly unidimensional; recent validations find two factors (illness likelihood and consequences). Reliability was still good (α 0.81).

Q: What is measurement invariance (Table 5)?

Testing in steps whether the scale works the same in Arabic and English: same structure, then same loadings, then same intercepts. CSS-12 passed all three; SHAI-14 passed the first two.

Q: Why is high cyberchondria defined as > 33?

There is no validated clinical cut-off for the CSS-12, so we used the upper tertile of our sample, as is common.

Q: Why is the prevalence so dependent on the cut-off (Table 4)?

Because most scores cluster just below the thresholds; that is why we report several cut-offs and say “elevated”, not “disorder”.

Q: What does Table 13 show?

That the main correlation stays 0.46–0.48 whatever we exclude, and the regression is stable. Only the AI effect depended on a decision.
