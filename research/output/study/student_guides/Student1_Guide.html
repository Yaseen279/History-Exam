Part 1 · The whole study (everyone must know this)

The committee can ask any of you about any part. You split the presentation, not the knowledge. Learn this part so well that you can explain the whole study without slides.

The study in two minutes (practise saying this out loud)

“Many people now look up their symptoms online before seeing a doctor, and more and more of them ask AI chatbots like ChatGPT. For some people this searching makes them more worried, not less. That is called cyberchondria, and it is closely linked to health anxiety. No one had studied this in Baghdad, and AI chatbots had hardly been studied next to search engines. We ran an online survey of 300 adults in Baghdad between 17 July 2026 and 11 September 2026, using two established scales: the SHAI-14 for health anxiety and the CSS-12 for cyberchondria. We found that AI chatbots are now the main source of health information: 78.0% ticked them, more than search engines (56.3%). About one in five (19%) had elevated health anxiety. Health anxiety and cyberchondria were moderately linked, r = 0.47, and this held after adjusting for everything else. The link ran mainly through excessive searching and post-search distress, which fits a vicious cycle, although one survey cannot prove direction. We found no reliable evidence that AI itself adds to cyberchondria beyond health anxiety. And searching shapes the consultation: about a third delay care because they feel reassured, and about a third never tell their doctor what they searched. So our main message for clinicians is simple: ask patients, without judgement, what they searched or asked an AI.”

Terms you must be able to explain in one sentence

TermPlain explanation
Health anxietyPersistent worry about having or developing a serious illness, kept going by misreading normal body sensations and by reassurance seeking.
Illness anxiety disorderThe DSM-5 diagnosis at the severe end of health anxiety. We did not diagnose it; we measured symptoms.
CyberchondriaExcessive or repeated online health searching that increases rather than relieves distress.
SHAI-1414 items, each scored 0–3 (total 0–42); higher = more health anxiety. ≥ 18 = elevated (screening threshold).
CSS-1212 items scored 1–5 (total 12–60) with four 3-item facets: excessiveness, compulsion, distress, reassurance seeking.
Cross-sectionalEverything measured once, at one time point: shows associations, not cause and effect.
Convenience / snowball samplingPeople who were easy to reach, who then shared the link with others. Quick, but not representative.
Mean ± SDThe average score and how spread out the scores are.
Median (IQR)The middle score and the range of the middle half; used when data are skewed.
p-valueHow surprising the result would be if there were truly no effect; p < 0.05 is called significant.
95% CIThe range in which the true value probably lies; narrower = more precise.
Correlation rHow closely two scores move together (0 = none, 1 = perfect). 0.47 is moderate.
Partial correlationThe same link after removing the effect of other variables.
Mann–Whitney / Kruskal–WallisNon-parametric tests comparing two / three or more groups, used because scores were not normally distributed.
χ² testTests whether two categorical variables are related (e.g., social-media use and elevated health anxiety).
FDR (q-value)Correction for running many tests; a result is “robust” if q < 0.05, otherwise exploratory.
Linear regression, B and βPredicts a score from several factors at once. B = change in CSS points per unit; β = the same on a standardised scale (to compare factors).
R² and ΔR²Share of variation explained by the model; ΔR² = extra share explained by adding a block of variables.
Logistic regression, AORPredicts a yes/no outcome. Adjusted odds ratio: how many times the odds change, holding other factors constant.
AUCHow well a model separates two groups (0.5 = chance, 1 = perfect).
MediationSplitting a link into a part that runs through a third variable (indirect) and the rest (direct). Statistical, not causal.
ModerationWhether a third variable changes the strength of a link (e.g., does AI use make the link stronger? No).
Cronbach α / McDonald ωInternal consistency: do the items of a scale hang together? ≥ 0.7 is acceptable, ≥ 0.8 good.
CFA (CFI, RMSEA, SRMR)Checks the items group into the intended factors. Good: CFI ≈ 0.95+, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, SRMR ≤ 0.08.
Measurement invarianceChecks the scale measures the same thing in two groups (here Arabic vs English) before comparing them.
Sensitivity analysisRe-running the main analysis in a different way to see if the result holds.
Never say (overclaims the committee will catch):
  • “Health anxiety causes cyberchondria” → say “is associated with”, “is compatible with a cycle”.
  • “19% have health anxiety disorder” → “19% scored in the elevated range on a screening tool”.
  • “AI makes people anxious” → “we found no reliable independent effect of AI use”.
  • “Our results represent Baghdad” → “a young, online, convenience sample; associations were consistent across subgroups”.
  • “The Arabic version is validated” → “we tested it: equal reliability and formal invariance, but no formal back-translation”.

Part 2 · Your chapter, section by section

For each section: its purpose, the key points and numbers (with the reference numbers used in the thesis), and what to watch.

1.1 Background

Why the topic matters.

  • Searching before seeing a doctor is now ordinary; it helps, but for some people it amplifies worry: cyberchondria [1][2].
  • Iraq is highly connected: about four in five Iraqis online by 2025 [3], yet nothing had been studied in Baghdad.

Watch: Why Baghdad? Why now? Your answer: high Internet use, rising AI use, no local data.

1.2 Health anxiety

What health anxiety is and why it matters.

  • A continuum from normal worry to illness anxiety disorder / somatic symptom disorder (DSM-5) [4].
  • Cognitive-behavioural model [5]: sensations misread → anxiety → checking and reassurance → short relief → worry returns.
  • Common and costly: ≈ 6% lifetime, up to 20% in hospital outpatients, unnecessary contacts [6][7]; rising over three decades [8].
  • Iraq: about half of 1591 online respondents reported health anxiety during COVID-19 [9]; home quarantine raised it in 1578 adults [10].

Watch: Be able to draw the cognitive cycle on a whiteboard.

1.3 Cyberchondria

Definition and measurement.

  • White & Horvitz [1]: benign searches escalate to rare, serious diagnoses. Starcevic & Berle [2]: searching that increases distress.
  • Not pathological in itself; becomes cyberchondria when excessive, compulsive, distressing [11]; possible transdiagnostic compulsive syndrome [12].
  • CSS-33 had five factors incl. mistrust of doctors [13]; CSS-12 keeps four [14]; Arabic CSS-12 validated in Lebanon [15].

Watch: Know why we used CSS-12 (short, four facets, Arabic version exists) and that it drops “mistrust”.

1.4 Health anxiety and cyberchondria

Why the two are studied together.

  • Related but distinct; cyberchondria adds impairment and healthcare use [16]. Searching raises anxiety in an experiment [17].
  • Meta-analyses: r = 0.34 with general online searching but 0.62 with cyberchondria [18]; 0.63, facets 0.24–0.66 [19].
  • Intolerance of uncertainty linked to cyberchondria (r = 0.55) [20].
  • Longitudinal: Dutch 4-wave panel of 5322 adults, reciprocal link [21]; pregnancy study, health anxiety as risk factor [22].
  • COVID-19 studies in Croatia and Turkey [23][24]; scoping review prevalence 30.7–55.6% [25].

Watch: The meta-analytic 0.62 will come back in the Discussion: our 0.47 is lower. Know that Student 3 explains why.

1.5 From search engines to AI chatbots

What is new about AI.

  • LLM chatbots are fluent, personal, repeatable; answers can seem more empathetic than doctors' [26][27].
  • US survey: ≈ 1 in 5 used ChatGPT for health; ≈ a third changed medication or asked for referrals [28].
  • Health anxiety predicts symptom-checker use [29]; Czech sample: link via trust in AI [30]; Turkish dermatology: 61% used AI before the visit [31].

Watch: Open question you can state: does AI relieve worry (calm answers) or feed it (endless reassurance)?

1.6 Searching and the doctor–patient relationship

Why behaviour matters clinically.

  • Discussing searches can help the relationship; hiding them strains it [32][33].
  • Arab region: Jeddah [34], Saudi primary care [35], 13 MENA countries: trust in online information predicted delayed consultation [36].

1.7 Rationale

Three gaps.

  • First CSS-12 data from Baghdad.
  • AI chatbots studied separately from search engines.
  • Psychology linked to real pre-consultation behaviour.

Watch: Practise saying the three gaps in one breath.

1.8 Aim, objectives, hypotheses, Figure 1

What the study set out to do.

  • Aim: relationship between health anxiety and cyberchondria and their correlates among adults in Baghdad, with attention to AI chatbots and pre-consultation behaviour.
  • H1: health anxiety ↔ cyberchondria (mainly distress, excessiveness). H2: independent of other factors. H3: excessive searching mediates HA → distress. H4: cyberchondria ↔ care-seeking and strained relationship.
  • H1–H2 primary; H3–H4 secondary; AI comparisons, item-level, moderation and sensitivity analyses exploratory.
  • Figure 1 shows these on the cycle.

Watch: Know which hypotheses were supported: H1 yes, H2 yes, H3 statistically (not causally), H4 partially.

2.1 Design and setting

  • Analytical cross-sectional; STROBE [37] and CHERRIES [38]; online, Baghdad, 17 July 2026 – 11 September 2026.

2.2 Participants and sampling

  • Convenience + snowball via Baghdad-based social-media and messaging groups.
  • Inclusion: ≥ 18 years, reads Arabic or English, informed consent.
  • All submissions retained; no duplicates; response rate cannot be calculated (snowball).
  • Three responses had been annotated by the team: kept, and excluded in a sensitivity analysis.

Watch: Residence in Baghdad was not asked directly: say so honestly.

2.3 Sample size

  • Fisher's z: r = 0.20, α 0.05, 80% power → 194 (85 for r = 0.30) [39]. 300 collected; ±5.7% margin for percentages (vs ±5% for 385).
  • Logistic model for elevated health anxiety: 57 events / 11 predictors ≈ 5 per variable (limitation).

2.4 Instrument

  • Bilingual Google Form, five parts (Figure 2). SHAI-14 [40], validated widely [41][42][43], Arabic version in Saudi Arabia [44].
  • CSS-12 [14]: 1–5 scale, four subscales.
  • Arabic wording by the team, not formal cross-cultural adaptation [45]; published Arabic versions [15][46][44] not used.
  • SHAI item 2 options displayed in a different order; scored by meaning; dropping it changed nothing.

Watch: This is the most attacked section. Prepare the Arabic-version answer (Part 5).

2.5 Variables

  • Elevated health anxiety: SHAI ≥ 18 (vs healthy controls, Swedish study) [42]; also 15, 22, 29.
  • High cyberchondria: CSS > 33 (upper tertile; no validated cut-off).
  • AI user: ticked AI or named a chatbot (32 named without ticking); analyses repeated with the strict definition.
  • Health background = medical, other health-science students and healthcare workers.

2.6 Statistical analysis

You name the methods; Student 2 explains them in depth.

  • Python 3 (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels, pingouin, factor_analyzer, semopy), fully scripted; SPSS file provided.
  • Psychometrics: α, ω [47], CFA by ML (criteria [48]), invariance by multi-group CFA [49] and DIF by ordinal logistic regression [50].
  • Correlations; Mann–Whitney/Kruskal–Wallis; χ²; FDR [52]; hierarchical robust regression; logistic and ordinal; bootstrap mediation [53]; moderation; sensitivity analyses.
  • Figure 2 summarises the study flow.

2.7 Ethics

  • Voluntary, anonymous, e-consent with age confirmation before any question; no names or contacts.

Part 4 · Your slides, one by one

The script below is the speaker-notes text of your slides in the presentation. Practise until you can say it in your own words without reading.

Slide 1 · An online cross-sectional survey

Good morning. We are [names], supervised by [supervisor]. Our research is titled: Health anxiety, AI chatbot use and cyberchondria among adults in Baghdad. In simple words: what happens when worried people search their symptoms online, and now ask AI chatbots like ChatGPT, before they see a doctor.

Slide 2 · is my chest pain a heart attack?

We open with two lines. The hadith reminds us that every illness has a cure, so seeking knowledge about health is good. The second line, from Montaigne, describes health anxiety exactly: the fear of illness is itself a kind of suffering. Our study sits between these two ideas: looking for information can help, or it can feed fear.

Slide 3 · Objectives

Our aim was to study the relationship between health anxiety and cyberchondria among adults in Baghdad who search for health information online. We had four objectives. First, describe how people search, including AI chatbots. Second, measure health anxiety and cyberchondria and how strongly they are linked. Third, look for pathways that fit a vicious cycle. Fourth, see how all this affects what people do before and during the consultation.

Slide 4 · Introduction

We will start with the background: what health anxiety is, what cyberchondria is, how they may feed each other, and why AI chatbots change the picture.

Slide 5 · Health Anxiety

Health anxiety is a persistent worry about having a serious illness. It is a spectrum: most people have some health worry; at the far end it becomes illness anxiety disorder or somatic symptom disorder in DSM-5. The cognitive-behavioural model says normal sensations are misread as signs of disease, and safety behaviours such as checking and reassurance seeking give short relief but keep the worry going. It is common and costly. In Iraq, about half of online respondents reported health anxiety during COVID. We measured it with the Short Health Anxiety Inventory, SHAI-14.

Slide 6 · Cyberchondria

The word cyberchondria comes from White and Horvitz, who analysed search logs and found that searches for common, benign symptoms often escalated to rare and serious diagnoses. Starcevic and Berle defined it as excessive or repeated online health searching that increases rather than relieves distress. Searching itself is normal; it becomes cyberchondria when it is excessive, compulsive and distressing. We measured it with the CSS-12, which has four parts: excessiveness, compulsion, distress and reassurance seeking.

Slide 7 · The Vicious Cycle

Why study these together? Meta-analyses show health anxiety correlates strongly with cyberchondria, around r = 0.62. Health-anxious people feel worse after searching, while people with low anxiety feel relieved. A Dutch study of 5322 adults over four waves suggested the relationship goes both ways. This is the cycle on the slide: anxiety drives searching, searching drives distress, distress drives reassurance seeking, and reassurance does not last. We tested whether our data fit this cycle.

Slide 8 · From “Dr Google” to “Dr ChatGPT”

Almost all the evidence comes from the search-engine era. AI chatbots are different: they answer fluently and personally, can be asked again and again, and can sound more empathetic than doctors. In a US survey about one in five people used ChatGPT for health information, and about a third changed medications or asked for referrals based on its answers. No study had compared AI chatbots with search engines in Iraq.

Slide 9 · Methodology

Now the methodology: design, participants, sample size, the questionnaire and the analysis.

Slide 10 · Study Design & Settings

This was an analytical cross-sectional study, reported using the STROBE and CHERRIES checklists. Data were collected online in Baghdad from 17 July 2026 to 11 September 2026. We used convenience and snowball sampling through social media and messaging groups based in Baghdad. Inclusion: 18 or older, able to read Arabic or English, and gave consent. Participation was anonymous and voluntary, with electronic consent before any question.

Slide 11 · Sample Size & Questionnaire

Sample size. Our main question is a correlation. With alpha 0.05 and 80% power we need 194 people to detect a small-to-moderate correlation of 0.20. We collected 300, and the expected correlation from meta-analyses is much bigger, so the study is well powered (power about 94%). The questionnaire was a bilingual Google Form with five parts: consent and demographics; digital health seeking including AI chatbots; the SHAI-14; the CSS-12; and behaviour before the consultation and the relationship with the doctor. Every item was shown in Arabic and English.

Slide 12 · Statistical Analysis

For the analysis we used Python, with every step scripted so it can be reproduced, and we also prepared an SPSS file. We checked reliability and factor structure, then correlations, group comparisons, multivariable regression, and mediation with bootstrapping. Because we ran many tests, we corrected p-values with the false discovery rate method, and we labelled anything that did not survive as exploratory. Now my colleague will present the results.

Part 5 · Questions and model answers

A. Questions on your part

Q: Why did you choose this topic?

Give your real reason (e.g., patients or relatives using ChatGPT before seeing a doctor). Then: no data from Iraq, and AI chatbots had not been studied next to search engines.

Q: What is the difference between health anxiety and cyberchondria?

Health anxiety is the underlying worry about illness; cyberchondria is a behaviour pattern, excessive searching that makes the worry worse. They overlap strongly but are distinct.

Q: Why is your study design cross-sectional?

Feasible for a student project and appropriate for associations. We say clearly it cannot show direction, and we recommend longitudinal studies.

Q: Your sample is not representative. Why convenience sampling?

There is no sampling frame for online adults in Baghdad. We describe the sample honestly, do not generalise percentages, and show associations were consistent across subgroups.

Q: Explain your sample size calculation.

Our primary outcome is a correlation. Fisher's z with α 0.05 and 80% power needs 194 participants to detect r = 0.20. We had 300; meta-analytic r is ≈ 0.62, so the calculation is conservative.

Q: Why not use Cochran's formula (385)?

That is for estimating a proportion with ±5% precision. Our primary aim was a correlation. For percentages, 300 gives ±5.7%, close to ±5%.

Q: Why SHAI-14 and CSS-12?

Both are widely used, short, reliable and have Arabic versions in the literature. CSS-12 has four facets, which let us test the cycle.

Q: Why SHAI ≥ 18?

It best separated people with health anxiety from healthy controls in the Swedish diagnostic study [42]. It is a screening threshold, so we say “elevated”, and Table 4 shows other cut-offs.

Watch out: Never call it a diagnosis.

Q: Did you translate the questionnaire properly?

No formal forward–backward translation, which is a limitation. But we tested equivalence: equal reliability, equal means, full scalar invariance of the CSS-12, metric invariance of the SHAI-14 and no item DIF.

Q: Why didn't you use the published Arabic versions?

We were not aware of them when the form was built [or give your real reason]. We cite them and recommend them for future work.

Watch out: Do not invent a reason; if unsure, say it was an oversight in planning.

Q: How do you know participants lived in Baghdad?

Through Baghdad-based distribution channels; there was no residence question. This is listed as a limitation.

Q: Could one person answer twice?

[Fill in: did Google Forms limit one response per account?] We found no duplicate response patterns.

Q: Why is it called “analytical” cross-sectional?

Because we tested associations and hypotheses, not just described prevalence.

Q: What are STROBE and CHERRIES?

Reporting checklists: STROBE for observational studies, CHERRIES for online surveys. They make sure we report everything a reader needs.

Q: What ethical issues did you consider?

Voluntary participation, anonymity, informed consent before any question, right to withdraw, secure data. [Approval number.]

Q: What are your hypotheses and were they supported?

H1 and H2 supported; H3 supported statistically but not causally; H4 only partially (only disclosure survived FDR).

Q: Why include AI chatbots?

Because they are new and now dominant: 78.0% ticked them. Earlier studies treated “the Internet” as one thing.

Q: Summarise your study in one minute.

Use the two-minute script in Part 1, shortened: design, sample, two scales, r = 0.47, AI result, behaviour, clinical message.

Q: What is your main finding?

Health anxiety and cyberchondria are moderately and robustly associated (r = 0.47), independent of other factors.

Q: What did you find about AI chatbots?

They are the main source (78.0% ticked), but there was no reliable independent effect on cyberchondria beyond health anxiety.

Q: What percentage had elevated health anxiety?

19% at SHAI ≥ 18, a screening threshold, not a diagnosis.

Q: What is your biggest limitation?

The cross-sectional design: no direction of cause.

Ask patients, without judgement, what they searched or asked an AI.

C. Difficult or hostile questions

Q: “This is just a student survey; why should we trust it?”

Calmly: established scales, good reliability, pre-specified hypotheses, correction for multiple testing, robust methods and sensitivity analyses; and we state our limitations openly.

Q: “You found what everyone already knows.”

The link itself is known; what is new is Baghdad data, AI chatbots studied separately, and the link to concrete behaviour before the consultation.

Q: “Why should a doctor care about this?”

Because a third of patients delay care when reassured online and a third hide their searches: both affect diagnosis and trust.
