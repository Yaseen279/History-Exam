2 · The AI-definition mistake (the 32 participants)

What happened, in plain words

  1. In Q8 people ticked the tools they use. In Q9 they named the chatbot they use.
  2. 32 people named a chatbot in Q9 (e.g. ChatGPT) but did not tick “AI chatbots” in Q8.
  3. Main analysis (inclusive): they were counted as AI users, correctly, because they named one. → 266 users vs 34 non-users.
  4. The check (strict definition) moved them into the non-user group. That was the mistake: people who named ChatGPT are not non-users. And they had high cyberchondria (median 34), so they made the “non-users” look worse and hid the difference.
  5. The fix: exclude the 32 from the check instead of moving them. → 234 users vs 34 non-users.
AI users / non-usersAdjusted effect of AI on cyberchondriaResult
Inclusive (main analysis)266 / 34B = 5.33 (95% CI 2.57–8.09), p < 0.001Significant
Strict (old check, 32 put in non-users)234 / 66B = 1.49 (−0.85–3.83), p = 0.21Not significant
Corrected check (32 excluded)234 / 34B = 4.38 (95% CI 1.68–7.08), p = 0.002Significant

Unadjusted with the 32 excluded: cyberchondria 29.2 vs 24.0, Mann–Whitney p < 0.001; high cyberchondria 28.6% vs 8.8%, p = 0.012.

Say it

We made a mistake in the sensitivity check: 32 people who named a chatbot were counted as non-users. When we excluded them instead, the association between AI use and cyberchondria stayed significant, B = 4.38, p = 0.002. So AI use is associated with higher cyberchondria, independently of health anxiety.

If she asks

Why did you exclude them instead of keeping the strict definition?

Because they named a chatbot, so they are not non-users. Putting them in the non-user group was misclassification. Excluding unclear cases is the standard way to check that a result does not depend on them.

Isn’t this just changing the analysis to get significance?

No: the main analysis was already significant (p < 0.001), and it does not change. Only the check was corrected, for a clear reason (misclassification), and we report it transparently.

What changes in the thesis?

2.4 (the definition paragraph describes exclusion instead of reclassification), 3.5 and 4.3 (the result), and the Abstract and Conclusion: AI use is associated with higher cyberchondria.

Why do those 32 have high cyberchondria?

Probably chance. Nothing about naming a chatbot without ticking a box should change cyberchondria; it only mattered because the non-user group is small (34).

Your decision before the meeting

Ask her approval to update 2.4, 3.5, 4.3, the Abstract and Conclusion 4 with the corrected result. She wrote those paragraphs, so she should agree first.

Why medians (her point, and she is right)

Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis are rank tests, used because the scores are not normally distributed. Rank tests go with the median (IQR): the middle person and the range of the middle half. The p-values do not change; only how each group is described. Our scores are only mildly skewed, so medians are close to the means.

4 · The whole study in two pages (backup)

PartThe essentials
AimRelationship between health anxiety and cyberchondria among adults in Baghdad, focusing on AI chatbots and medical students.
DesignAnalytical cross-sectional online survey, bilingual Google Form, convenience + snowball sampling, 17 July – 11 September 2026, n = 300, one response per Google account, no missing data.
ToolsSHAI-14 (health anxiety, 0–42, ≥ 18 elevated, Swedish cut-off) and CSS-12 (cyberchondria, 12–60, four dimensions: excessiveness, compulsion, distress, reassurance seeking).
HypothesesH1: more health anxiety → more cyberchondria, independently of other factors. H2: medical students have higher health anxiety and cyberchondria.
StatisticsSPSS 26, p < 0.05. Shapiro–Wilk (not normal) → Mann–Whitney / Kruskal–Wallis; χ² / Fisher with Cramér’s V; Pearson + Spearman, partial r; hierarchical multiple linear regression; binary logistic regression.
SampleYoung (62.3% aged 20–29), 56.3% female, 44.0% medical students.
SearchingAI chatbots 88.7% (78.0% ticked), search engines 56.3%, ChatGPT 59.3%. After searching: 34.7% delayed care because reassured; 31.7% never tell the doctor.
LevelsSHAI mean 12.35, median 12; 19.0% elevated (95% CI 15.0–23.8). CSS mean 28.86, median 29. α 0.81 and 0.86.
H1Supported. r = 0.47 (0.38–0.55), partial r = 0.48; regression: health anxiety adds 18.4%, β = 0.48 (strongest predictor).
H2Not supported. Medical students: higher SHAI before adjustment (median 12.5 vs 11, p = 0.004) but not after adjusting for age (p = 0.081); CSS identical.
AIAI users: higher cyberchondria, adjusted B = 5.33 (p < 0.001); holds with the 32 unclear cases excluded (B = 4.38, p = 0.002).
LogisticElevated health anxiety: cyberchondria AOR 3.12 per SD, reassurance motive 3.83, older age 0.61 (protective). AUC 0.83.
LimitsCross-sectional (no direction), convenience sample (not generalisable), no back-translation, self-report, many tests without correction.

Questions she might ask about the whole study

If she asks

Why non-parametric tests?

Shapiro–Wilk showed the scores were not normally distributed, so Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis instead of the t-test and ANOVA.

Which groups differ in Table 6?

Kruskal–Wallis shows only that groups differ overall. Post-hoc (Mann–Whitney with Bonferroni): 18–19 vs 30–39 (p = 0.026), students vs bachelor graduates (p = 0.032), medical students vs non-health employees (p = 0.004): younger students are more health-anxious.

Why is H2 not supported if medical students scored higher?

Because the difference disappeared after adjusting for age: medical students are younger, and younger people had higher health anxiety.

What does r = 0.47 mean?

A moderate link: health anxiety explains about 22% of the differences in cyberchondria.

Why SHAI ≥ 18?

It best separated people with health anxiety from healthy controls in the Swedish validation study.

What is the main clinical message?

Ask patients, without judgement, what they searched or asked an AI, and what they found.
