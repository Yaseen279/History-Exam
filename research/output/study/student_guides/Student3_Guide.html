Part 1 · The whole study (everyone must know this)

The committee can ask any of you about any part. You split the presentation, not the knowledge. Learn this part so well that you can explain the whole study without slides.

The study in two minutes (practise saying this out loud)

“Many people now look up their symptoms online before seeing a doctor, and more and more of them ask AI chatbots like ChatGPT. For some people this searching makes them more worried, not less. That is called cyberchondria, and it is closely linked to health anxiety. No one had studied this in Baghdad, and AI chatbots had hardly been studied next to search engines. We ran an online survey of 300 adults in Baghdad between 17 July 2026 and 11 September 2026, using two established scales: the SHAI-14 for health anxiety and the CSS-12 for cyberchondria. We found that AI chatbots are now the main source of health information: 78.0% ticked them, more than search engines (56.3%). About one in five (19%) had elevated health anxiety. Health anxiety and cyberchondria were moderately linked, r = 0.47, and this held after adjusting for everything else. The link ran mainly through excessive searching and post-search distress, which fits a vicious cycle, although one survey cannot prove direction. We found no reliable evidence that AI itself adds to cyberchondria beyond health anxiety. And searching shapes the consultation: about a third delay care because they feel reassured, and about a third never tell their doctor what they searched. So our main message for clinicians is simple: ask patients, without judgement, what they searched or asked an AI.”

Terms you must be able to explain in one sentence

TermPlain explanation
Health anxietyPersistent worry about having or developing a serious illness, kept going by misreading normal body sensations and by reassurance seeking.
Illness anxiety disorderThe DSM-5 diagnosis at the severe end of health anxiety. We did not diagnose it; we measured symptoms.
CyberchondriaExcessive or repeated online health searching that increases rather than relieves distress.
SHAI-1414 items, each scored 0–3 (total 0–42); higher = more health anxiety. ≥ 18 = elevated (screening threshold).
CSS-1212 items scored 1–5 (total 12–60) with four 3-item facets: excessiveness, compulsion, distress, reassurance seeking.
Cross-sectionalEverything measured once, at one time point: shows associations, not cause and effect.
Convenience / snowball samplingPeople who were easy to reach, who then shared the link with others. Quick, but not representative.
Mean ± SDThe average score and how spread out the scores are.
Median (IQR)The middle score and the range of the middle half; used when data are skewed.
p-valueHow surprising the result would be if there were truly no effect; p < 0.05 is called significant.
95% CIThe range in which the true value probably lies; narrower = more precise.
Correlation rHow closely two scores move together (0 = none, 1 = perfect). 0.47 is moderate.
Partial correlationThe same link after removing the effect of other variables.
Mann–Whitney / Kruskal–WallisNon-parametric tests comparing two / three or more groups, used because scores were not normally distributed.
χ² testTests whether two categorical variables are related (e.g., social-media use and elevated health anxiety).
FDR (q-value)Correction for running many tests; a result is “robust” if q < 0.05, otherwise exploratory.
Linear regression, B and βPredicts a score from several factors at once. B = change in CSS points per unit; β = the same on a standardised scale (to compare factors).
R² and ΔR²Share of variation explained by the model; ΔR² = extra share explained by adding a block of variables.
Logistic regression, AORPredicts a yes/no outcome. Adjusted odds ratio: how many times the odds change, holding other factors constant.
AUCHow well a model separates two groups (0.5 = chance, 1 = perfect).
MediationSplitting a link into a part that runs through a third variable (indirect) and the rest (direct). Statistical, not causal.
ModerationWhether a third variable changes the strength of a link (e.g., does AI use make the link stronger? No).
Cronbach α / McDonald ωInternal consistency: do the items of a scale hang together? ≥ 0.7 is acceptable, ≥ 0.8 good.
CFA (CFI, RMSEA, SRMR)Checks the items group into the intended factors. Good: CFI ≈ 0.95+, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, SRMR ≤ 0.08.
Measurement invarianceChecks the scale measures the same thing in two groups (here Arabic vs English) before comparing them.
Sensitivity analysisRe-running the main analysis in a different way to see if the result holds.

Part 2 · Your chapter, section by section

For each section: its purpose, the key points and numbers (with the reference numbers used in the thesis), and what to watch.

4.1 Principal findings

The four headline findings.

  • First CSS-12 study in Baghdad; among the first to put AI chatbots next to search engines.
  • (1) AI used by 78.0%–88.7%; (2) r = 0.47, independent; (3) concentrated in excessiveness and distress; (4) searching shapes the consultation.

Watch: This is your opening slide content (slide 25).

4.2 Health anxiety and cyberchondria

Why our r is weaker than meta-analyses.

  • Ours 0.47 vs 0.62–0.63 [18][19].
  • Reasons: measurement error (corrected ≈ 0.56); low, narrow health anxiety (mean 12.3); online recruitment of confident users; CSS-12 instead of the 33-item CSS.
  • 19% elevated is a screening estimate; 7%–33% across cut-offs; consistent with high pandemic health anxiety in Iraq [9].

Watch: Learn the four reasons as a numbered list.

4.3 Do they fuel each other?

  • Consistent with the cognitive model and the Dutch longitudinal study [5][21].
  • But cross-sectional mediation is biased for longitudinal processes [54]; an indirect effect cannot identify the true order [55]; shared method variance [56].
  • H3 supported statistically, not causally.

Watch: If you remember one sentence: “compatible with a cycle, not proof of direction”.

4.4 AI chatbots

  • Our 78–89% vs ≈ 20% in a 2024 US survey [28] and 21% vs 98% search engines in 2025 [57].
  • Young sample: 96% at 18–19 y vs 72% at ≥ 40 y.
  • Inclusive AI effect not robust; no moderation; absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.
  • Trust → fewer multiple-doctor visits but a trend to reassured delay (OR 1.33, p 0.088), echoing MENA data [36].

4.5 The consultation

  • Reassured delay (34.7%): saves visits for minor symptoms, risks delayed diagnosis; occurs at all anxiety levels.
  • Cyberchondria with urgent care (exploratory), mirroring healthcare use [6][16].
  • Non-disclosure 31.7%; higher cyberchondria → more disclosure (robust); health anxiety → concealment (exploratory); discussed searches strengthen the relationship [33].
  • H4 partially supported.

4.6 Medical students

  • Higher unadjusted health anxiety disappeared after adjustment (age). SHAI–CSS r 0.57 in medical students vs 0.40 others.
  • “Medical student syndrome”: weak evidence; a Palestinian study with the same SHAI ≥ 18 found medical students lowest [58].
  • Medical knowledge did not protect against cyberchondria.

Watch: Expect a question here: you are medical students yourselves.

4.7 Regional comparison

  • Jeddah: common, linked with smartphone addiction [34]; Saudi primary care: linked with health anxiety [35]; Lebanon: Arabic CSS-12 structure similar [15].
  • Egypt: 1435 medical students, 85.8% moderate cyberchondria [59]; Saudi CSS-12 validation [46].

4.8 Strengths and limitations

  • Strengths: established scales, bilingual with invariance testing, AI separate, complete data, FDR, robust and bootstrap methods, sensitivity analyses, links to behaviour.
  • Limitations: cross-sectional; convenience sample (young, 44% medical students); Arabic wording without back-translation; single-item behaviour measures; self-report; many tests and small subgroups; AI classification; careless responding.

Watch: For each limitation, know what was done about it (Part 5).

5.1 Conclusions (six)

  • AI chatbots a leading source (78.0% / 88.7%).
  • 19% elevated (screening; 0.7% at 29).
  • r = 0.47, strongest predictor.
  • Compatible with a cycle, direction unproven.
  • No reliable independent AI effect.
  • Searching shapes the consultation: delay 34.7%, non-disclosure 31.7%, strained trust 34.7%.

5.2 Recommendations

  • Clinicians: ask non-judgementally; recognise repeated searching as a sign; screen with SHAI; CBT strategies; teach digital/AI literacy in medical school.
  • Ministry of Health: Arabic digital and AI health-literacy campaigns; reliable Arabic triage sources.
  • AI developers: anxiety-aware answers, no escalation to rare diagnoses, Arabic.
  • Research: interventions (only 9 registered cyberchondria studies, none in Iraq [60]); longitudinal and experimental designs; probability sampling; adapted Arabic scales.

Watch: Recommendations must follow from results: be ready to link each to a finding.

Slide 24 · Discussion

Thank you. I will discuss what these results mean, how they compare with other studies, our limitations, and our conclusions.

Slide 25 · n = 300 · r = 0.47

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use the CSS-12 in Baghdad, and among the first anywhere to look at AI chatbots next to search engines before seeing a doctor. Our main finding, r = 0.47, supports our first two hypotheses: health anxiety and cyberchondria go together, independently of everything else we measured.

Slide 26 · Comparison with Worldwide Results

Now, how do our results compare with studies from around the world?

Slide 27 · Comparison with Worldwide Results

Our correlation of 0.47 is weaker than the pooled estimates of about 0.62 from meta-analyses. Several reasons: first, measurement error; corrected for reliability it would be about 0.56, close to the meta-analytic range. Second, our sample had low health anxiety, and a narrow range weakens correlations. Third, online recruitment may favour confident, habitual internet users. Fourth, most earlier studies used the full 33-item scale.

Slide 28 · Comparison with Regional Studies

Our findings agree with the Arab region. In Jeddah, cyberchondria was common and linked with smartphone addiction. In Saudi primary care, cyberchondria was linked with health anxiety. In Lebanon, the Arabic CSS-12 had the same structure as ours. And among 1435 Egyptian medical students, the group closest to ours, most had moderate cyberchondria.

Slide 29 · AI Chatbots & the Consultation

AI use in our sample, 78.0% to 88.7%, is far higher than in a 2024 US survey, about 1 in 5, and a 2025 survey, 21%. Our sample is young; AI use fell from 96% at 18 to 19 years to 72% at 40 and above. On the consultation: delaying care because of reassurance can save visits for minor symptoms, but risks delayed diagnosis for serious ones, and it happened across all anxiety levels. About a third never tell their doctor what they searched. So doctors should ask.

Slide 30 · 44.0% of our sample were medical students, a distinct group:

44.0% of our sample were medical students, a distinct group: high medical awareness, academic stress, and exposure to descriptions of serious disease. They had higher unadjusted health anxiety, but this disappeared after adjustment, because they are younger, and age was the independent factor. Their cyberchondria scores were identical to everyone else's. So medical knowledge did not protect against cyberchondria.

Slide 31 · Limitations

We have several limitations. First, the design is cross-sectional, so we cannot prove direction; the mediation is statistical, not causal. Second, the sample is online convenience and snowball, young, and heavy in medical students, so percentages cannot be generalised to all of Baghdad, although associations were consistent across subgroups. Third, the Arabic wording was ours, without formal back-translation, although reliability and means were the same in both languages. Fourth, behaviour items were single, non-validated questions. Fifth, many tests; we corrected with FDR.

Slide 32 · Future Directions & Recommendations

For the future: longitudinal studies to test the cycle over time; experiments comparing AI chatbot and search-engine answers; probability sampling of all Baghdad adults; and formally adapted Arabic scales. ClinicalTrials.gov lists only nine cyberchondria studies and none in Iraq, so intervention trials are a clear gap. For practice: doctors should ask about searches; the Ministry may consider digital and AI health-literacy campaigns; and AI developers may consider anxiety-aware answers.

Slide 33 · Conclusion

To conclude. AI chatbots, mainly ChatGPT, are now the main source of health information before seeing a doctor in our sample. About one in five has elevated health anxiety. Health anxiety and cyberchondria are moderately and robustly linked, r = 0.47, compatible with a cycle. We found no reliable independent effect of AI. And searching shapes the consultation: delays, non-disclosure and strained trust. Thank you.

Part 5 · Questions and model answers

A. Questions on your part

Q: Why is your correlation weaker than other studies?

Four reasons: measurement error (corrected r ≈ 0.56), low and narrow health anxiety, online recruitment of confident users, and the short CSS-12.

Q: Is your sample representative of Baghdad?

No: young, educated, many medical students. We do not generalise percentages; the associations were consistent across subgroups.

Q: What is new in your study?

First CSS-12 data from Baghdad; AI chatbots separated from search engines; psychology linked to behaviour before the consultation.

Q: Why is AI use so high compared with the US?

A young, educated, health-oriented sample; AI use fell clearly with age (96% → 72%). It is not an estimate for all adults.

Q: So does AI make cyberchondria worse?

We found no reliable independent effect beyond health anxiety. That is absence of evidence, not evidence of absence; experiments are needed.

Q: Is delaying care because of reassurance good or bad?

Both: it may prevent unnecessary visits for minor symptoms but risks missing serious ones. It occurred at all anxiety levels.

Q: Why do people with more cyberchondria disclose more?

Searching is central to their behaviour and they bring findings as part of reassurance seeking; health-anxious patients may hide searches for fear of being seen as “worried well” (exploratory).

Q: Are medical students more anxious?

Unadjusted yes, but it disappeared after adjustment (younger age). Their cyberchondria was the same as others'. A Palestinian study [58] found medical students lowest.

Q: What is your biggest limitation?

Cross-sectional design: no causal direction. Second, the convenience sample.

Q: If you repeated the study, what would you change?

Probability sampling across ages, formal Arabic adaptation (or the published versions), a clearer AI-use question, and follow-up over time.

Q: How do your findings compare with the Arab region?

Consistent: Jeddah, Saudi primary care and Lebanon all link cyberchondria with health anxiety; Egyptian medical students show high cyberchondria.

Q: What should a doctor do differently on Monday?

Ask every patient, without judgement, what they searched or asked an AI; treat repeated searching and reassurance seeking as signs of health anxiety; avoid endless reassurance.

Q: What should the Ministry do?

Support Arabic digital and AI health-literacy campaigns and reliable Arabic triage sources, warning especially about false reassurance.

Q: Why recommend intervention trials?

ClinicalTrials.gov lists only nine cyberchondria studies, none in Iraq; brief Arabic digital or CBT interventions are a clear gap.

Q: Was H4 supported?

Partially: only higher cyberchondria → more disclosure survived FDR; urgent care and strained relationship were exploratory.

Q: How do you know the results are not due to careless responders?

We excluded straight-liners and annotated responses in sensitivity analyses; the correlation did not change (Table 13).

Q: What does “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence” mean here?

Not finding a robust AI effect does not prove AI has no effect; our design and the small non-user group could miss it.

Q: Why is the Discussion careful with words like “cycle”?

Because one survey cannot show direction; we use “compatible with” to stay within what the data show.

C. Difficult or hostile questions

Q: “Your recommendations go beyond your data.”

Each one is tied to a finding (e.g., ask about searches ← 31.7% never disclose); policy recommendations are phrased as “may consider”.

Q: “Why should we believe a self-report survey?”

Self-report is standard for these constructs; the scales are validated, reliable and invariant; we acknowledge recall and social-desirability bias.

Q: “Isn't the AI finding just a coding decision?”

Yes, and we say so openly; that is why we conclude there is no reliable evidence of an independent AI effect.

Part 6 · Editing your chapter

The Word file is the master copy. Edit only the .docx, in one shared OneDrive / Word Online file with Review → Track Changes on. Keep an untouched copy (Thesis_ORIGINAL.docx). Avoid Google Docs: it breaks the automatic numbering.

For your chapter

  • Discussion is where your own interpretation belongs: rewrite each paragraph in your voice, keeping every [n] with its claim.
  • Add genuine local insight (how patients in Baghdad use ChatGPT, pharmacies, cost of visits) only if it is true.
  • Do not strengthen claims: keep “compatible with”, “exploratory”, “may consider”.
  • Recommendations can be personal and practical, but each must follow from a result.
  • Keep the limitations complete: removing one makes the thesis look less honest, not stronger.

Never touch

  • Numbers, %, p, q, CI, r, OR, β, n
  • Reference numbers like [18] (move them with their sentence)
  • The reference list
  • Table/figure captions, table of contents and lists (automatic fields)
  • Heading numbers and styles
  • Times New Roman 14 and page numbering

The rewrite method (paragraph by paragraph)

  1. Read until you understand it; look up anything unclear.
  2. Close it and explain it out loud.
  3. Write your version from that explanation.
  4. Compare: same facts, numbers and [n]; nothing claimed more strongly.
  5. Read aloud; change any phrase you would never say.

After editing

  1. Ctrl + A, then F9, to update the table of contents, the lists and the captions.
  2. Search for “[” to find placeholders left.
  3. Swap chapters with a teammate for checking.
  4. Export to PDF and look at every page.
About AI use, honestly. Check your college's AI policy with your supervisor and declare AI assistance if required. A sentence you can adapt: “The statistical analysis code and an initial draft of this report were prepared with the assistance of an AI tool (Claude, Anthropic). All analyses were checked, and the text was reviewed and rewritten by the authors, who take full responsibility for its content.” Do not use paraphrasing tools or tricks to fool detectors: they make the text worse and can count as misconduct. Real ownership (your words, your understanding, your edit history) is the best protection.
