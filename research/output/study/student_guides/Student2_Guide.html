Part 1 · The whole study (everyone must know this)

The committee can ask any of you about any part. You split the presentation, not the knowledge. Learn this part so well that you can explain the whole study without slides.

The study in two minutes (practise saying this out loud)

“Many people now look up their symptoms online before seeing a doctor, and more and more of them ask AI chatbots like ChatGPT. For some people this searching makes them more worried, not less. That is called cyberchondria, and it is closely linked to health anxiety. No one had studied this in Baghdad, and AI chatbots had hardly been studied next to search engines. We ran an online survey of 300 adults in Baghdad between 17 July 2026 and 11 September 2026, using two established scales: the SHAI-14 for health anxiety and the CSS-12 for cyberchondria. We found that AI chatbots are now the main source of health information: 78.0% ticked them, more than search engines (56.3%). About one in five (19%) had elevated health anxiety. Health anxiety and cyberchondria were moderately linked, r = 0.47, and this held after adjusting for everything else. The link ran mainly through excessive searching and post-search distress, which fits a vicious cycle, although one survey cannot prove direction. We found no reliable evidence that AI itself adds to cyberchondria beyond health anxiety. And searching shapes the consultation: about a third delay care because they feel reassured, and about a third never tell their doctor what they searched. So our main message for clinicians is simple: ask patients, without judgement, what they searched or asked an AI.”

Median (IQR)The middle score and the range of the middle half; used when data are skewed.

Correlation rHow closely two scores move together (0 = none, 1 = perfect). 0.47 is moderate.
Partial correlationThe same link after removing the effect of other variables.

χ² testTests whether two categorical variables are related (e.g., social-media use and elevated health anxiety).

Part 2 · Your chapter, section by section

For each section: its purpose, the key points and numbers (with the reference numbers used in the thesis), and what to watch.

3.1 Participants and online health-information seeking (Tables 1–2, Figures 2–3)

Who answered, how they search, and what they do after searching.

  • 300 adults, no missing data; 62.3% aged 20–29; 56.3% female; medical students 44.0%; health background 63.3%; chronic condition 21.7%; Arabic 70.7%.
  • AI ticked 78.0% (88.7% incl. named) vs search engines 56.3%; AI without any search engine 41.0%; ChatGPT 59.3%. AI use 96% at 18–19 y vs 72% at ≥ 40 y.
  • Motives: convenience 80.0%, “do I need a doctor?” 57.7%, reassurance 51.0%.
  • After searching: delay/skip care when reassured 34.7%; ignore 30.0%; several doctors 25.3%; urgent care 22.0%. Always tell the doctor 27.0%; never (fear of judgement) 31.7%; relationship strained 34.7%.

Watch: Message: a young, digitally active sample whose first “doctor” is often an AI; searching changes the consultation.

3.2 Levels of health anxiety and cyberchondria (Tables 3–4)

  • SHAI 12.3 ± 5.8; elevated 19% (CI 15.0–23.8%); Table 4: 32.7% at ≥ 15, 19.0% at ≥ 18, 7.3% at ≥ 22, 0.7% at ≥ 29.
  • CSS 28.9 ± 8.4; excessiveness highest, compulsion lowest.
  • Cronbach α 0.81 (SHAI) and 0.86 (CSS): good. Arabic and English responders did not differ (Mann–Whitney p 0.86 and 0.65).

Watch: Why several cut-offs? Because prevalence depends on the threshold; reporting all four is honest.

3.3 Main association (Table 5, Figure 4)

The central result (H1, H2).

  • Pearson r = 0.47 (95% CI 0.38–0.55), Spearman ρ = 0.47, p < 0.001; explains 22%; partial r = 0.48.
  • Facets: strongest with excessiveness 0.46 and distress 0.45, weakest with compulsion 0.25 and reassurance 0.24.

Watch: H1 and H2 are answered here.

3.4 Factors associated (Tables 6–7, Figure 5)

Mann–Whitney / Kruskal–Wallis and χ² tests (p < 0.05).

  • Health anxiety higher with a chronic condition (p 0.004), in reassurance seekers (p < 0.001), social-media users (p 0.001) and AI users (p 0.008); differs by age (p 0.008), education (p 0.002) and occupation (p 0.005); gender and language no difference.
  • Cyberchondria higher in AI users (p < 0.001), highest in AI + search engine users (p < 0.001), and in social-media users (p 0.021).
  • Elevated health anxiety → 52.6% vs 23.0% high cyberchondria (OR 3.71) (Table 7).

Watch: Many tests at p < 0.05: a few weaker results could be chance (we say so in the limitations).

3.5 Independent predictors (Tables 8–9, Figure 6)

  • Hierarchical linear regression: R² 0.030 → 0.133 → 0.317; health anxiety adds 18.4%; B = 0.68, β = 0.48, p < 0.001. Ages 30–39 (B 6.3) and ≥ 40 (B 4.0) also higher than 20–29.
  • AI: +5.3 inclusive vs +1.5, p 0.21 strict → no reliable independent effect.
  • Binary logistic (57 events): AOR 3.12 cyberchondria per SD, 3.83 reassurance motive, 0.61 age; Nagelkerke R² 0.31; Hosmer–Lemeshow p 0.50 (good fit); AUC 0.83; ≈ 5 events per variable → estimates may be overestimated.
  • Sensitivity (one sentence): r stayed 0.46–0.48 in every check.

Watch: You will be asked about AOR, AUC and Hosmer–Lemeshow.

3.6 Behaviour and pathways (Tables 10–11, Figures 7–8)

  • Higher CSS → urgent visits (AOR 1.51, p 0.013), less ignoring (0.65, p 0.009), strained relationship (1.35, p 0.041) and more disclosure (never-disclose AOR 0.57, p 0.001).
  • Higher SHAI → concealment (never-disclose AOR 1.47, p 0.017). Trust → fewer multiple doctors (AOR 0.59, p 0.004).
  • Mediation (Sobel test): via excessive searching 0.16 (z 5.14, p < 0.001; about a third); via distress 0.22 (z 5.98), direct 0.01 (ns); reverse 0.15 (z 4.66).

Watch: Always end with: “compatible with a cycle, not proof of direction”.

Part 4 · Your slides, one by one

The script below is the speaker-notes text of your slides in the presentation. Practise until you can say it in your own words without reading.

Slide 13 · Results

Thank you. I will present the results in six steps: who took part, how they search, the levels of health anxiety and cyberchondria, the main association, the predictors and pathways, and finally behaviour and AI chatbots.

Slide 14 · Participants

Opening: Table 1 shows our 300 participants. Main finding: the sample is young, 62.3% aged 20 to 29, slightly more women at 56.3%, and 44.0% are medical students; 63.3% have a health-related background, and 21.7% reported a chronic condition. 70.7% answered mainly in Arabic. Closing: so this is a young, educated, digitally active sample, and that matters when we interpret percentages.

Slide 15 · How People Search

Opening: Figure 2 shows the tools people use and why. Main finding: AI chatbots are now the main gateway. 78.0% ticked AI chatbots, 88.7% if we include those who named one, compared with 56.3% for search engines. 41.0% use AI with no search engine at all, and ChatGPT alone is used by 59.3%. The top motives are convenience at 80.0%, checking whether a doctor is needed at 57.7%, and instant reassurance at 51.0%. Closing: in summary, for this sample, the first doctor is often an AI chatbot.

Slide 16 · Levels & Reliability

Opening: Table 3 shows the scores and reliability. Main finding: mean SHAI was 12.3 out of 42 and 19% scored 18 or more, our threshold for elevated health anxiety, with a 95% confidence interval of 15.0–23.8%. Mean CSS was 28.9 out of 60. Excessiveness was the highest facet and compulsion the lowest: people search a lot, but it rarely disrupts their lives. Both scales were reliable, alpha 0.81 and 0.86, and scores did not differ between Arabic and English responders. Closing: our tools measured well in this population.

Slide 17 · Health Anxiety ↔ Cyberchondria

Opening: this is our main result, Figure 4. Main finding: health anxiety and cyberchondria rise together, r = 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.55, so health anxiety explains about 22% of the variation in cyberchondria. After adjusting for every other variable, the partial correlation is still 0.48. The link is strongest for excessive searching, 0.46, and distress, 0.45, and weaker for compulsion and reassurance. Closing: in summary, hypotheses 1 and 2 are supported.

Slide 18 · What Predicts Cyberchondria?

Opening: Table 8 is the hierarchical regression for cyberchondria. Main finding: demographics alone explain only about 3% of cyberchondria. Adding digital-use variables brings it to 13%. Adding health anxiety adds another 18.4%, to about 32%. Health anxiety is the strongest predictor, standardised beta 0.48: each extra SHAI point adds about 0.68 CSS points. Closing: who you are matters little; how anxious you are matters most.

Slide 19 · Item by Item: What Drives Searching?

Opening: this supplementary figure breaks the association down item by item, all 168 pairs. Main finding: the strongest links involve fear of serious illness and interpreting body sensations; for example fear of illness with panicking when reading about a rare condition. Simply noticing aches and pains was almost unrelated. Closing: what drives searching is fear and interpretation, not just noticing symptoms, exactly what the cognitive model predicts.

Slide 20 · Who Has Elevated Health Anxiety?

Opening: Figure 6 shows who has elevated health anxiety, in a multivariable logistic model. Main finding: three factors are independent. Each standard deviation of cyberchondria triples the odds, adjusted odds ratio 3.12. Searching for instant reassurance almost quadruples them, 3.83. Older age is protective, 0.61 per age step. The model fits well, Hosmer–Lemeshow p 0.50, and discriminates well, AUC 0.83. Closing: the person most at risk is young and searches to feel reassured.

Slide 21 · Pathways: Is There a Cycle?

Opening: Figure 7 shows the pathways. Main finding: in model A, part of the link from health anxiety to post-search distress runs through excessive searching, indirect effect 0.16, Sobel z 5.14, p below 0.001. In model B, post-search distress carries almost all of the link from health anxiety to reassurance-seeking visits: the direct effect drops to 0.01. But the reverse direction also fits, 0.15. Closing: our data are compatible with the cycle, but one survey at one time point cannot prove direction. Hypothesis 3 is supported statistically, not causally.

Slide 22 · Behaviour Before the Consultation

Opening: Figure 3 shows what people do after searching. Main finding: the most common response is to delay or skip care because they feel reassured, 34.7%. Others escalate: urgent visits 22.0%, multiple doctors 25.3%. Only 27.0% always tell their doctor about their searches, and 31.7% never do, for fear of being judged. Higher cyberchondria was linked with more disclosure, odds ratio 0.57, and also with urgent visits and a strained relationship. 34.7% say searching complicated or reduced trust in their doctor. Closing: searching changes the consultation before it begins.

Slide 23 · AI Chatbots: An Independent Effect?

Opening: finally, did AI chatbots themselves matter? Main finding: AI users scored 5.3 points higher on cyberchondria after adjustment, mainly excessiveness and reassurance. But 32 people named a chatbot without ticking the AI option. When we use the strict definition, only people who ticked it, the effect disappears: B = 1.5, p = 0.21. And only 34 people were true non-users. Closing: no reliable independent effect of AI. Absence of evidence, not evidence of absence. I now hand over to my colleague for the discussion.

Part 5 · Questions and model answers

A. Questions on your part

Q: Why non-parametric tests?

Shapiro–Wilk showed the scores were not normally distributed; Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis do not assume normality.

Q: Explain r = 0.47 to a non-statistician.

People with more health anxiety clearly tend to have more cyberchondria; anxiety accounts for about a fifth (22%) of the differences.

Q: Why both Pearson and Spearman?

Pearson for the linear correlation, Spearman as a check that does not depend on normality; both were 0.47.

Q: What does partial r = 0.48 tell us?

The link is not explained by age, gender, background, chronic disease, trust or AI use: it survives adjustment (H2).

Q: Why hierarchical regression?

To see what health anxiety adds after demographics and digital use: 18.4% extra variance, by far the biggest block.

Q: What do B and β mean in Table 8?

B = 0.68: each SHAI point adds about 0.7 CSS points. β = 0.48: the standardised effect, used to compare predictors.

Q: What is an adjusted odds ratio of 3.12?

For each standard deviation higher cyberchondria, the odds of elevated health anxiety are about three times higher, holding other factors constant.

Q: Why is the reassurance-motive AOR so wide?

Only 57 events for 11 predictors (≈ 5 per variable), so estimates are imprecise and probably optimistic; we interpret them cautiously.

Q: Does the mediation prove a vicious cycle?

No. One time point, the reverse order fits too, and the facets come from one questionnaire. It is compatible with a cycle; only longitudinal data can show direction.

Q: Why does the AI effect disappear?

It depends on 32 people who named a chatbot without ticking it; they had the highest scores. Under the strict definition p = 0.21. Only 34 non-users made a small comparison group.

Q: Chronic illness was not significant in the logistic model. Why?

Dichotomising SHAI at 18 loses information and power. On the continuous score chronic illness is significant: p = 0.004 unadjusted and p = 0.029 adjusted (B +1.7).

Q: Why is high cyberchondria defined as > 33?

There is no validated clinical cut-off for the CSS-12, so we used the upper tertile of our sample, as is common.

Q: Why is the prevalence so dependent on the cut-off (Table 4)?

Because most scores cluster just below the thresholds; that is why we report several cut-offs and say “elevated”, not “disorder”.

Q: Which software did you use?

IBM SPSS Statistics [version]; the syntax file reproduces every table (say this only after the team has run it and checked the numbers).

Q: Why didn't you correct for multiple testing?

We used the standard p < 0.05, as most graduation theses do, and we say in the limitations that with many tests some weaker results may be chance. Our main results are p < 0.001.

Q: What is the AUC?

The area under the ROC curve: how well the model separates elevated from non-elevated anxiety. 0.83 is good.

Q: What does the Hosmer–Lemeshow test tell you?

Whether the logistic model fits the data. We want it non-significant: p = 0.50, so the fit is good.

Q: How did you test mediation?

Three linear regressions per model (paths a, b, c and c′) and the Sobel test for the indirect effect a × b.

Q: What does Sobel z = 5.14 mean?

The indirect effect is about five standard errors above zero, so p < 0.001: part of the link runs through excessive searching, statistically.

Q: Why Mann–Whitney and not the t-test?

Shapiro–Wilk showed the scores were not normal (p < 0.01), so we used non-parametric tests that compare ranks.

Use the two-minute script in Part 1, shortened: design, sample, two scales, r = 0.47, AI result, behaviour, clinical message.

C. Difficult or hostile questions

Q: “Your p-values are tiny because your sample is large.”

The effect sizes are meaningful too: r = 0.47 is moderate and ΔR² 18.4% is a medium effect.

Q: “You ran so many tests; something will be significant.”

Yes, that is possible for the weaker results, and we say so in the limitations. The main findings (r = 0.47, health anxiety in the regression, the mediation paths) have p < 0.001 and would survive any correction.

Q: “The mediators are subscales of the same scale. Isn't that circular?”

Partly, yes: shared method variance inflates the paths. That is why we call it a statistical decomposition, not a mechanism.
