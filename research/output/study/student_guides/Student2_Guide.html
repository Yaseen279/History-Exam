Part 1 · The whole study (everyone must know this)

The committee can ask any of you about any part. You split the presentation, not the knowledge. Learn this part so well that you can explain the whole study without slides.

The study in two minutes (practise saying this out loud)

“Many people now look up their symptoms online before seeing a doctor, and more and more of them ask AI chatbots like ChatGPT. For some people this searching makes them more worried, not less. That is called cyberchondria, and it is closely linked to health anxiety. No one had studied this in Baghdad, and AI chatbots had hardly been studied next to search engines. We ran an online survey of 300 adults in Baghdad between 17 July 2026 and 11 September 2026, using two established scales: the SHAI-14 for health anxiety and the CSS-12 for cyberchondria. We found that AI chatbots are now the main source of health information: 78.0% ticked them, more than search engines (56.3%). About one in five (19%) had elevated health anxiety. Health anxiety and cyberchondria were moderately linked, r = 0.47, and this held after adjusting for everything else. The link ran mainly through excessive searching and post-search distress, which fits a vicious cycle, although one survey cannot prove direction. We found no reliable evidence that AI itself adds to cyberchondria beyond health anxiety. And searching shapes the consultation: about a third delay care because they feel reassured, and about a third never tell their doctor what they searched. So our main message for clinicians is simple: ask patients, without judgement, what they searched or asked an AI.”

Terms you must be able to explain in one sentence

TermPlain explanation
Health anxietyPersistent worry about having or developing a serious illness, kept going by misreading normal body sensations and by reassurance seeking.
Illness anxiety disorderThe DSM-5 diagnosis at the severe end of health anxiety. We did not diagnose it; we measured symptoms.
CyberchondriaExcessive or repeated online health searching that increases rather than relieves distress.
SHAI-1414 items, each scored 0–3 (total 0–42); higher = more health anxiety. ≥ 18 = elevated (screening threshold).
CSS-1212 items scored 1–5 (total 12–60) with four 3-item facets: excessiveness, compulsion, distress, reassurance seeking.
Cross-sectionalEverything measured once, at one time point: shows associations, not cause and effect.
Convenience / snowball samplingPeople who were easy to reach, who then shared the link with others. Quick, but not representative.
Mean ± SDThe average score and how spread out the scores are.
Median (IQR)The middle score and the range of the middle half; used when data are skewed.
p-valueHow surprising the result would be if there were truly no effect; p < 0.05 is called significant.
95% CIThe range in which the true value probably lies; narrower = more precise.
Correlation rHow closely two scores move together (0 = none, 1 = perfect). 0.47 is moderate.
Partial correlationThe same link after removing the effect of other variables.
Mann–Whitney / Kruskal–WallisNon-parametric tests comparing two / three or more groups, used because scores were not normally distributed.
χ² testTests whether two categorical variables are related (e.g., social-media use and elevated health anxiety).
FDR (q-value)Correction for running many tests; a result is “robust” if q < 0.05, otherwise exploratory.
Linear regression, B and βPredicts a score from several factors at once. B = change in CSS points per unit; β = the same on a standardised scale (to compare factors).
R² and ΔR²Share of variation explained by the model; ΔR² = extra share explained by adding a block of variables.
Logistic regression, AORPredicts a yes/no outcome. Adjusted odds ratio: how many times the odds change, holding other factors constant.
AUCHow well a model separates two groups (0.5 = chance, 1 = perfect).
MediationSplitting a link into a part that runs through a third variable (indirect) and the rest (direct). Statistical, not causal.
ModerationWhether a third variable changes the strength of a link (e.g., does AI use make the link stronger? No).
Cronbach α / McDonald ωInternal consistency: do the items of a scale hang together? ≥ 0.7 is acceptable, ≥ 0.8 good.
CFA (CFI, RMSEA, SRMR)Checks the items group into the intended factors. Good: CFI ≈ 0.95+, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, SRMR ≤ 0.08.
Measurement invarianceChecks the scale measures the same thing in two groups (here Arabic vs English) before comparing them.
Sensitivity analysisRe-running the main analysis in a different way to see if the result holds.
Never say (overclaims the committee will catch):
  • “Health anxiety causes cyberchondria” → say “is associated with”, “is compatible with a cycle”.
  • “19% have health anxiety disorder” → “19% scored in the elevated range on a screening tool”.
  • “AI makes people anxious” → “we found no reliable independent effect of AI use”.
  • “Our results represent Baghdad” → “a young, online, convenience sample; associations were consistent across subgroups”.
  • “The Arabic version is validated” → “we tested it: equal reliability and formal invariance, but no formal back-translation”.

Who answered.

3.2 Online health-information seeking (Table 2, Figure 3)

How people search.

  • AI ticked 78.0% (88.7% incl. named) vs search engines 56.3%; AI without any search engine 41.0%; ChatGPT 59.3%.
  • AI use 96% at 18–19 y, 72% at ≥ 40 y; the 34 non-users are older.
  • Motives: convenience 80.0%, “do I need a doctor?” 57.7%, reassurance 51.0%; trust mostly moderate.

Watch: Message: for this sample, the first “doctor” is often an AI.

3.3 Behaviour and the doctor relationship (Figure 4)

  • Delay/skip care when reassured 34.7%; ignore 30.0%; several doctors 25.3%; urgent care 22.0%.
  • Always tell the doctor 27.0%; never (fear of judgement) 31.7%; relationship complicated or trust reduced 34.7%.

Watch: The “never” option combined frequency and a reason: say “never tell, citing fear of judgement”.

3.4 Levels and psychometrics (Tables 3, 4, 5)

  • SHAI 12.3 ± 5.8; elevated 19% (CI 15.0–23.8%); Table 4: 32.7% at ≥ 15, 19.0% at ≥ 18, 7.3% at ≥ 22, 0.7% at ≥ 29.
  • CSS 28.9 ± 8.4; excessiveness 9.66 (highest), compulsion 5.00 (lowest).
  • Reliability α 0.81 / 0.86; ω 0.82 / 0.87. CSS-12 four-factor CFA (ML) CFI 0.95, RMSEA 0.070, SRMR 0.053; SHAI one-factor poor (CFI 0.83): not strictly unidimensional.
  • Table 5: CSS-12 configural → metric (Δχ² 5.52, p 0.70, ΔCFI +0.002) → scalar (Δχ² 8.41, p 0.40, ΔCFI 0.000): fully invariant. SHAI-14 metric supported (p 0.73), scalar not (p 0.007, ΔCFI −0.018). No item DIF after FDR.

Watch: Explain why prevalence depends on the cut-off and why that is not a weakness: it is honest reporting.

3.5 Main association (Table 6, Figures 5, 6)

The central result.

  • r = 0.47 (95% CI 0.38–0.55), ρ = 0.47, explains 22%; partial r = 0.48.
  • Facets: excessiveness 0.46, distress 0.45 (stronger, Williams' t p < 0.001) vs compulsion 0.25, reassurance 0.24.
  • Item level: fear of serious illness and interpretation of sensations matter, mere awareness does not; 111/168 pairs survive FDR (58 Holm); strongest ρ 0.43 (Figure 11, Appendix A).

Watch: H1 and H2 are answered here.

3.6 Bivariate factors (Tables 7, 8, Figure 7)

162 tests.

  • Health anxiety higher with chronic condition (p 0.004, q 0.028) and reassurance motive; age/education/occupation borderline (exploratory); gender no difference.
  • AI users higher CSS (inclusive), mainly excessiveness and reassurance; highest in AI + search engine users; chatbot brand no difference.
  • χ²: elevated HA → 52.6% vs 23.0% high cyberchondria (OR ≈ 3.7).

Watch: Remember q-values: “survives correction” vs “exploratory”.

3.7 Multivariable models (Tables 9, 10, Figure 8)

  • Hierarchical regression: R² 0.030 → 0.133 → 0.317; health anxiety adds 18.4% (f² medium); B = 0.68, β = 0.48.
  • AI: +5.3 (CI 2.3–8.3) inclusive vs +1.5, p 0.31 strict; the 32 reclassified had the highest CSS (31.6 vs 24.0 in non-users).
  • Reverse model: CSS strongest predictor of SHAI; chronic disease B +1.7 (p 0.029); medical students no longer differ after adjustment.
  • Logistic (57 events): AOR 3.12 cyberchondria per SD, 3.83 reassurance motive, 0.61 age; AUC 0.83 → 0.79; ≈ 5 events per variable → exploratory.

Watch: You will be asked to explain AOR, AUC and why it was corrected.

3.8 Behaviour models (Table 11)

  • Cyberchondria (not health anxiety) goes with urgent visits (AOR 1.51), less ignoring (0.65), strained relationship (ordinal OR 1.34).
  • Only robust behavioural result: higher CSS → lower odds of never disclosing (AOR 0.57, q 0.012). Health anxiety → concealment AOR 1.47, q 0.098 (exploratory).
  • Trust → fewer multiple-doctor visits (AOR 0.59).

Watch: H4 only partially supported.

3.9 Pathways (Table 12, Figures 9, 10)

  • HA → excessive searching → distress: indirect 0.16 (CI 0.11–0.23), direct 0.30.
  • HA → distress → reassurance seeking: indirect 0.22 (CI 0.16–0.29), direct 0.01 (ns).
  • Reverse path also significant (0.15); serial path 0.07. No moderator significant after FDR; age trend p 0.07.

Watch: Always end with: “compatible with a cycle, not proof of direction”.

3.10 Sensitivity (Table 13)

  • Excluding annotated (r 0.46) or straight-liners (r 0.47), dropping SHAI item 2 (r 0.48), bootstrap CI for B 0.54–0.83, quantile B 0.55–0.85, no quadratic effect (p 0.25), VIF ≤ 3.9, early vs late responders no difference; residuals non-normal → robust SEs.
  • Only the AI effect depended on a decision (definition).

Slide 13 · Results

Thank you. I will present the results in six steps: who took part, how they search, the levels of health anxiety and cyberchondria, the main association, the predictors and pathways, and finally behaviour and AI chatbots.

Slide 14 · Participants

Opening: Table 1 shows our 300 participants. Main finding: the sample is young, 62.3% aged 20 to 29, slightly more women at 56.3%, and 44.0% are medical students; 63.3% have a health-related background, and 21.7% reported a chronic condition. 70.7% answered mainly in Arabic. Closing: so this is a young, educated, digitally active sample, and that matters when we interpret percentages.

Slide 15 · How People Search

Opening: Figure 3 shows the tools people use and why. Main finding: AI chatbots are now the main gateway. 78.0% ticked AI chatbots, 88.7% if we include those who named one, compared with 56.3% for search engines. 41.0% use AI with no search engine at all, and ChatGPT alone is used by 59.3%. The top motives are convenience at 80.0%, checking whether a doctor is needed at 57.7%, and instant reassurance at 51.0%. Closing: in summary, for this sample, the first doctor is often an AI chatbot.

Slide 16 · Levels & Reliability

Opening: Table 3 shows the scores and reliability. Main finding: mean SHAI was 12.3 out of 42 and 19% scored 18 or more, our threshold for elevated health anxiety, with a 95% confidence interval of 15.0–23.8%. Mean CSS was 28.9 out of 60. Excessiveness was the highest facet and compulsion the lowest: people search a lot, but it rarely disrupts their lives. Both scales were reliable, alpha 0.81 and 0.86, and reliability was the same in Arabic and English responders. Formal invariance testing showed the CSS-12 works the same way in both languages, and no single item behaved differently. Closing: our tools measured well in this population.

Slide 17 · Health Anxiety ↔ Cyberchondria

Opening: this is our main result, Figure 5. Main finding: health anxiety and cyberchondria rise together, r = 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.55, so health anxiety explains about 22% of the variation in cyberchondria. After adjusting for every other variable, the partial correlation is still 0.48. The link is strongest for excessive searching, 0.46, and distress, 0.45, and weaker for compulsion and reassurance. Closing: in summary, hypotheses 1 and 2 are supported.

Slide 18 · What Predicts Cyberchondria?

Opening: Table 9 is the hierarchical regression for cyberchondria. Main finding: demographics alone explain only about 3% of cyberchondria. Adding digital-use variables brings it to 13%. Adding health anxiety adds another 18.4%, to about 32%. Health anxiety is the strongest predictor, standardised beta 0.48: each extra SHAI point adds about 0.68 CSS points. Closing: who you are matters little; how anxious you are matters most.

Slide 19 · Item by Item: What Drives Searching?

Opening: Figure 11 breaks the association down item by item, all 168 pairs. Main finding: 111 pairs remained significant after FDR correction. The strongest links involve fear of serious illness and interpreting body sensations; for example fear of illness with panicking when reading about a rare condition. Simply noticing aches and pains was almost unrelated. Closing: what drives searching is fear and interpretation, not just noticing symptoms, exactly what the cognitive model predicts.

Slide 20 · Who Has Elevated Health Anxiety?

Opening: Figure 8 shows who has elevated health anxiety, in a multivariable logistic model. Main finding: three factors are independent. Each standard deviation of cyberchondria triples the odds, adjusted odds ratio 3.12. Searching for instant reassurance almost quadruples them, 3.83. Older age is protective, 0.61 per age step. The model discriminates well: AUC 0.83, 0.79 after correcting for optimism. Closing: the person most at risk is young and searches to feel reassured.

Slide 21 · Pathways: Is There a Cycle?

Opening: Figure 9 shows the pathways. Main finding: in model A, part of the link from health anxiety to post-search distress runs through excessive searching, indirect effect 0.16, CI 0.11–0.23. In model B, post-search distress carries almost all of the link from health anxiety to reassurance-seeking visits: the direct effect drops to 0.01. But the reverse direction also fits, 0.15. Closing: our data are compatible with the cycle, but one survey at one time point cannot prove direction. Hypothesis 3 is supported statistically, not causally.

Slide 22 · Behaviour Before the Consultation

Opening: Figure 4 shows what people do after searching. Main finding: the most common response is to delay or skip care because they feel reassured, 34.7%. Others escalate: urgent visits 22.0%, multiple doctors 25.3%. Only 27.0% always tell their doctor about their searches, and 31.7% never do, for fear of being judged. Higher cyberchondria was linked with more disclosure, odds ratio 0.57, and this survived FDR correction. 34.7% say searching complicated or reduced trust in their doctor. Closing: searching changes the consultation before it begins.

Slide 23 · AI Chatbots: An Independent Effect?

Opening: finally, did AI chatbots themselves matter? Main finding: AI users scored 5.3 points higher on cyberchondria after adjustment, mainly excessiveness and reassurance. But 32 people named a chatbot without ticking the AI option. When we use the strict definition, only people who ticked it, the effect disappears: B = 1.5, p = 0.31. And only 34 people were true non-users. AI use also did not change the strength of the anxiety–cyberchondria link. Closing: no reliable independent effect of AI. Absence of evidence, not evidence of absence. I now hand over to my colleague for the discussion.

Part 5 · Questions and model answers

A. Questions on your part

Q: Why non-parametric tests?

Shapiro–Wilk showed the scores were not normally distributed; Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis do not assume normality. Welch tests confirmed the same conclusions.

Q: What is FDR and why did you use it?

With 162 comparisons, about 8 would be “significant” by chance at p < 0.05. Benjamini–Hochberg controls the proportion of false discoveries; results with q ≥ 0.05 are labelled exploratory.

Q: Explain r = 0.47 to a non-statistician.

People with more health anxiety clearly tend to have more cyberchondria; anxiety accounts for about a fifth (22%) of the differences.

Q: Why both Pearson and Spearman?

Pearson for the linear correlation, Spearman as a check that does not depend on normality; both were 0.47.

Q: What does partial r = 0.48 tell us?

The link is not explained by age, gender, background, chronic disease, trust or AI use: it survives adjustment (H2).

Q: Why hierarchical regression?

To see what health anxiety adds after demographics and digital use: 18.4% extra variance, by far the biggest block.

Q: What do B and β mean in Table 9?

B = 0.68: each SHAI point adds about 0.7 CSS points. β = 0.48: the standardised effect, used to compare predictors.

Q: Why robust (HC3) standard errors?

The residuals were heteroskedastic (Breusch–Pagan p = 0.004); HC3 gives valid standard errors anyway.

Q: What is an adjusted odds ratio of 3.12?

For each standard deviation higher cyberchondria, the odds of elevated health anxiety are about three times higher, holding other factors constant.

Q: Why is the reassurance-motive AOR so wide?

Only 57 events for 11 predictors (≈ 5 per variable), so estimates are imprecise and probably optimistic; we label them exploratory.

Q: What is the AUC and why corrected?

How well the model separates elevated from non-elevated anxiety. Models look better on their own data, so 500 bootstrap refits corrected 0.83 to 0.79.

Q: Does the mediation prove a vicious cycle?

No. One time point, the reverse order fits too, and the facets come from one questionnaire. It is compatible with a cycle; only longitudinal data can show direction.

Q: Why does the AI effect disappear?

It depends on 32 people who named a chatbot without ticking it; they had the highest scores. Under the strict definition p = 0.31. Only 34 non-users made a small comparison group.

Q: Chronic illness was not significant in the logistic model. Why?

Dichotomising SHAI at 18 loses information and power. On the continuous score chronic illness is significant: p = 0.004 unadjusted and p = 0.029 adjusted (B +1.7).

Q: What do CFI, RMSEA and SRMR mean?

Fit indices for CFA. CSS-12 four factors: CFI 0.95, RMSEA 0.070, SRMR 0.053: acceptable. SHAI one-factor fitted poorly.

Q: Why does the SHAI fit poorly?

It is not strictly unidimensional; recent validations find two factors (illness likelihood and consequences). Reliability was still good (α 0.81).

Q: What is measurement invariance (Table 5)?

Testing in steps whether the scale works the same in Arabic and English: same structure, then same loadings, then same intercepts. CSS-12 passed all three; SHAI-14 passed the first two.

Q: Why is high cyberchondria defined as > 33?

There is no validated clinical cut-off for the CSS-12, so we used the upper tertile of our sample, as is common.

Q: Why is the prevalence so dependent on the cut-off (Table 4)?

Because most scores cluster just below the thresholds; that is why we report several cut-offs and say “elevated”, not “disorder”.

Q: What does Table 13 show?

That the main correlation stays 0.46–0.48 whatever we exclude, and the regression is stable. Only the AI effect depended on a decision.

C. Difficult or hostile questions

Q: “Your p-values are tiny because your sample is large.”

The effect sizes are meaningful too: r = 0.47 is moderate and ΔR² 18.4% is a medium effect (f² ≈ 0.27).

Q: “You ran so many tests; something will be significant.”

Exactly why we used FDR correction and separated robust from exploratory findings.

Q: “The mediators are subscales of the same scale. Isn't that circular?”

Partly, yes: shared method variance inflates the paths. That is why we call it an exploratory decomposition, not a mechanism.
