Part B · Statistics from zero (with our numbers)

Each lesson: the idea in plain words, our example, and what to say. Read them in order; each one builds on the one before.

1. Variables: two kinds
  • Categorical = groups (gender, uses AI yes/no, tool profile). We describe them with n and %.
  • Continuous = scores on a scale (SHAI 0–42, CSS 12–60). We describe them with mean, SD, median.

This one fact decides almost every choice of test and chart in the thesis.

2. Mean and SD

Mean = the average. SD (standard deviation) = how spread out people are around the average. About two-thirds of people lie within 1 SD of the mean.

SHAI mean 12.3 ± SD 5.8 → most people scored between about 6.5 and 18
The average health-anxiety score was 12.3 out of 42.
3. Median and IQR

The median is the middle person. The IQR is the range of the middle half. We use them when scores are skewed (not a symmetrical bell shape).

SHAI median 12 (IQR 8–16): half of people scored 8 to 16
4. Normal distribution and why it matters

Many tests assume scores form a bell curve. The Shapiro–Wilk test checks this. Our scores were slightly skewed (p < 0.01), so we used non-parametric tests (they do not need the bell shape) and ordinary (multiple linear and logistic) regression.

Our scores were not perfectly normal, so we used tests that do not assume normality.
5. p-value

Imagine there is truly no link. The p-value is the chance of seeing a result as strong as ours just by luck. Small p (below 0.05) = "unlikely to be luck" = significant. It does not say how big or important the effect is.

r = 0.47, p < 0.001 → almost impossible to get this by chance if there were no link
p tells you if an effect exists, not how big it is. For size, look at r, β, OR.
6. 95% confidence interval (CI)

Our number is an estimate from 300 people. The CI is the range where the true value probably lies. Narrow CI = precise. If a CI for an odds ratio includes 1, the result is not significant.

r = 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.55 → the true correlation is probably between 0.38 and 0.55: always clearly above zero
7. Correlation r

How closely two scores move together: 0 = no link, 0.1 small, 0.3 moderate, 0.5 large, 1 = perfect. Positive = both go up together.

r = 0.47: people with more health anxiety tend to have more cyberchondria. r² = 22%: health anxiety explains about a fifth of the differences in cyberchondria.
Health anxiety and cyberchondria rise together: r = 0.47, a moderate link.
8. Pearson vs Spearman

Pearson r assumes a straight-line link; Spearman ρ uses ranks and does not need normal data. We report both; both were 0.47, so the result does not depend on the method.

9. Partial correlation

The same correlation after "removing" other variables (age, gender, background, chronic disease, trust, AI use). If it stays the same, those variables are not the explanation.

r = 0.47 → partial r = 0.48: unchanged
The link stayed the same after adjusting for everything else, so it is not explained by age, gender or background.
10. Comparing groups: Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis

To compare a score between groups without assuming normality: Mann–Whitney U for 2 groups, Kruskal–Wallis H for 3 or more.

Chronic condition: SHAI 14.2 vs 11.9 → Mann–Whitney p = 0.004
11. Chi-square (χ²)

To test if two categorical variables are related, e.g. "elevated health anxiety yes/no" and "uses social media yes/no". It compares the counts we saw with the counts we would expect if there were no link.

12. Many tests and chance findings

Like most graduation theses, we used the usual rule: p < 0.05 = significant. Because we ran many tests, a few “significant” results could be luck (with 100 tests at 0.05, about 5 would be). We did not adjust the p-values; instead we say so honestly in the limitations and we rely most on results that are strong (p < 0.001) and consistent across analyses.

We used p below 0.05; because we ran many tests, we mention in the limitations that some weaker results may be due to chance.
13. Linear regression, B and β

Regression predicts a score (CSS) from several factors at the same time, so each factor is "adjusted" for the others. B = how many CSS points change per 1 unit of the factor. β = the same on a common scale, so you can compare factors (bigger β = stronger).

SHAI: B = 0.68, β = 0.48. Someone with SHAI 20 vs 10 → about 6.8 CSS points higher.
Each extra point of health anxiety adds about 0.7 points of cyberchondria; it is by far the strongest factor.
14. R², ΔR² and hierarchical regression

R² = share of differences the model explains (0–1). In hierarchical regression we add groups of variables in steps and see how much each step adds (ΔR²).

Step 1 demographics R² 0.030 (3%) → step 2 digital use 0.133 (13%) → step 3 health anxiety 0.317 (32%). Health anxiety adds 18.4%.
Hierarchical order shows that health anxiety matters even after everything else is already in the model.
15. Checking the regression: VIF

If two predictors are almost the same thing, the regression cannot separate them (multicollinearity). SPSS gives the VIF for each predictor: below 5 is fine. Ours were all low.

16. Odds and odds ratios (logistic regression)

Logistic regression predicts a yes/no outcome (elevated health anxiety yes/no). It gives odds ratios (OR): OR 1 = no effect, above 1 = higher odds, below 1 = lower odds. Adjusted OR (AOR) = after accounting for the other factors.

Worked example from our data:
With elevated health anxiety: 52.6% had high cyberchondria → odds = 52.6/47.4 = 1.11
Without: 23.0% → odds = 23.0/77.0 = 0.30
Odds ratio = 1.11 / 0.30 ≈ 3.7

In the model: AOR 3.12 per 1 SD of cyberchondria (1 SD ≈ 8.4 CSS points) means about three times the odds for every 8 extra points.

AOR below 1 means protective. Age AOR 0.61: each older age group had lower odds.
17. AUC (ROC curve)

How well the logistic model separates people with and without elevated health anxiety. Pick one person with and one without: AUC is the chance the model gives the higher risk to the right one. 0.5 = coin toss, 0.7–0.8 acceptable, 0.8–0.9 good.

AUC 0.83: good
18. The Sobel test

Tests whether an indirect effect (a × b) is bigger than zero. You need a, its SE, b and its SE (from two regressions in SPSS): z = a×b / √(b²·SEa² + a²·SEb²). If |z| > 1.96, p < 0.05.

Model A: a = 0.46 (SE 0.05), b = 0.35 (SE 0.06) → Sobel z = 5.14, p < 0.001
19. Mediation (indirect effect)

Does part of the link from X to Y go through a middle variable M? Path a = X → M; path b = M → Y (adjusted for X); indirect = a × b; direct (c′) = what is left.

Model A: health anxiety → excessive searching → distress
a = 0.46, b = 0.35 → indirect = 0.46 × 0.35 ≈ 0.16
total = 0.46, direct = 0.30 → about 35% goes through excessive searching
Model C: health anxiety → distress → reassurance seeking: indirect 0.22, direct 0.01 → almost all goes through distress
Mediation in one survey is NOT proof of cause. The reverse order was also significant (0.15). Always say: compatible with a cycle, not proof.
20. Hosmer–Lemeshow test

Checks whether a logistic model fits the data: here we want a non-significant p (p > 0.05 = good fit).

Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.50 → good fit
21. Reliability: Cronbach α

Do the items of a scale measure the same thing? 0.7 acceptable, 0.8 good, 0.9 excellent.

SHAI α 0.81, CSS α 0.86: good
22. Fisher's exact test and Cramér's V

χ² needs enough people in each cell (expected count ≥ 5). When cells are small, Fisher's exact test gives the p-value instead. Cramér's V is the strength of a χ² association: 0.1 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large.

23. z-scores (standardised scores)

A z-score shows how far someone is from the average in SDs (0 = average, +1 = one SD above). We used them so odds ratios are “per 1 SD” and mediation coefficients are comparable. In SPSS: Descriptives → “Save standardized values”.

24. Where each test is in SPSS

Frequencies: Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Frequencies · Reliability: Analyze → Scale → Reliability Analysis · Correlation: Analyze → Correlate → Bivariate / Partial · Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis: Analyze → Nonparametric Tests → Legacy Dialogs · χ²: Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Crosstabs → Statistics · Linear regression: Analyze → Regression → Linear (Next for each block) · Logistic: Analyze → Regression → Binary Logistic · AUC: Analyze → Classify → ROC Curve. All of it is in our syntax file.

25. Sensitivity analysis

Re-doing the main analysis in another way (excluding straight-line responders, removing one item) to see if the result changes. Ours did not change: r stayed 0.47–0.48.

Table 1 · Who took part
Every study must describe its sample first, so the reader knows who the results apply to. A table, because the reader needs exact n and %.

How to read it:

  1. Rows = characteristics (age, gender, education, study/work, background, chronic disease, language).
  2. Columns = n (number of people) and %.

What it shows: A young sample (62.3% aged 20–29), 44.0% medical students, 63.3% with a health background.

Our 300 participants were mostly young and educated; almost half were medical students. This matters when we interpret percentages.
Why so many medical students? Answer: the link spread through our own networks first; we say percentages cannot be generalised.
Table 2 + Figure 2 · How people search and why
Tools and motives are categories with multiple answers, so percentages are best shown as bars (Figure 2); Table 2 keeps every exact count.

How to read it:

  1. Figure 2, panel A = tools; panel B = motives.
  2. Each bar = % of all 300 who ticked that option.
  3. Bars add up to more than 100% because people could tick several.

What it shows: AI chatbots 78.0% ticked (88.7% including those who named one) vs search engines 56.3%; convenience is the top motive.

AI chatbots are now the main gateway: 78.0% ticked them, compared with 56.3% for search engines.
Why two AI numbers? 32 people named a chatbot but did not tick “AI”; we report both and test both.
Figure 3 · What people do after searching
Three related sets of categories (behaviour, disclosure, relationship) shown side by side so the committee sees the whole consultation story in one picture.

How to read it:

  1. Panel A: behaviours after searching (multiple answers).
  2. Panel B: do they tell the doctor? (always / sometimes / never).
  3. Panel C: effect on the relationship with doctors.

What it shows: 34.7% delay care when reassured; 31.7% never tell the doctor; 34.7% say trust was strained.

Searching changes the consultation: one in three delays care because they feel reassured, and almost one in three never tells the doctor.
Table 3 · Scores and reliability
Before using any score, we must show the scales work (reliability) and give their levels. A table because these are exact values.

How to read it:

  1. Rows: SHAI, CSS and the four CSS facets.
  2. Columns: mean ± SD, median (IQR), range, Cronbach α.

What it shows: Both scales reliable (α 0.81 and 0.86); excessiveness is the highest facet, compulsion the lowest.

Both questionnaires were reliable. People search a lot, but it rarely disrupts their daily life.
Table 4 · Prevalence at different cut-offs
The % with “elevated” health anxiety depends on the cut-off you choose. Showing four cut-offs is honest and stops the committee from accusing us of choosing the one that looks best.

How to read it:

  1. Each row = a cut-off (15, 18, 22, 29).
  2. Columns: n, %, 95% CI.
  3. Bold row = 18, the one we use.

What it shows: 32.7% at 15, 19.0% at 18, 7.3% at 22, 0.7% at 29.

About one in five scored 18 or more; this is a screening threshold, not a diagnosis, and the table shows how the estimate changes with the cut-off.
Why 18? It best separated people with health anxiety from healthy controls in the Swedish study.
Table 5 + Figure 4 · The main result
This answers our main question (H1), so it gets both: a figure to see the pattern, a table for exact numbers. A scatter plot is the standard picture for two continuous scores.

How to read it:

  1. Each dot = one participant (x = SHAI, y = CSS).
  2. The red line = best-fit line; the shaded band = its 95% CI.
  3. The dotted vertical line = SHAI 18 cut-off.
  4. Table 5: r for the total and for each facet, plus adjusted β.

What it shows: Clear upward trend: r = 0.47; strongest for excessiveness (0.46) and distress (0.45).

This is our main result: the more health anxiety, the more cyberchondria, r = 0.47, and it holds after adjustment.
Point at the line going up, then at the r value in the box. Do not say “causes”.
Table 6 + Figure 5 · Which groups score higher
Table 6 compares the scores across every participant group (exact means and p). Figure 5 is a box plot because CSS is skewed: it shows the median and spread, not just the mean.

How to read it:

  1. Box plot: middle line = median; box = middle half of people; whiskers = range; dots = outliers.
  2. Three groups: no AI, AI only, AI + search engine.

What it shows: Health anxiety higher with chronic illness (14.2 vs 11.9) and in reassurance seekers (13.8 vs 10.9); CSS higher in AI users (29.5 vs 24.0), highest in AI + search engine users.

People who combine AI with search engines had the highest cyberchondria, but we will see this depends on how AI use is defined.
Do not over-sell the AI difference; it disappears in the strict analysis.
Table 7 · Yes/no outcomes (χ²)
Some outcomes are yes/no (elevated health anxiety, high cyberchondria). χ² tests show which categories go with them. Only significant ones are listed to keep it short.

How to read it:

  1. Each row: variable, % with the outcome in each group, Cramér's V (strength), p.

What it shows: With elevated health anxiety, 52.6% had high cyberchondria vs 23.0% without (OR ≈ 3.7).

People with elevated health anxiety were much more likely to have high cyberchondria.
Table 8 · What predicts cyberchondria (regression)
Group comparisons look at one factor at a time; regression tests all factors together, so we know which matter independently (H2).

How to read it:

  1. Rows = predictors; B = CSS points per unit; β = comparable strength; p.
  2. Note under the table = R² for each step.

What it shows: Health anxiety β 0.48 (largest by far); R² rises from 0.030 to 0.317.

Demographics explain only 3%; adding health anxiety explains a further 18.4%. Health anxiety is by far the strongest predictor.
AI row: significant here, but not with the strict definition (slide 23).
Table 9 + Figure 6 · Who has elevated health anxiety
For a yes/no outcome we use logistic regression; a forest plot is the standard way to show many odds ratios with CIs at once.

How to read it:

  1. Each dot = AOR; the line = its 95% CI.
  2. Vertical line at 1 = no effect. If the line crosses 1 → not significant.
  3. Log scale: 0.5 and 2 are the same distance from 1.
  4. Orange = significant.

What it shows: Cyberchondria AOR 3.12, reassurance motive 3.83, age 0.61 (protective); AUC 0.83.

Three factors stand out: higher cyberchondria, searching for reassurance, and younger age.
Only 57 events, so the reassurance odds ratio has a wide CI; say the estimates may be overestimated.
Table 10 · Behaviour models
To test H4 we need adjusted models for each behaviour. A table because there are 8 outcomes × 2 scores: too many for a chart.

How to read it:

  1. Each row = a behaviour; columns = AOR (95% CI) and p for SHAI and for CSS.

What it shows: Higher CSS → urgent visits (AOR 1.51), less ignoring (0.65), less never-disclosing (0.57) and a strained relationship (1.35); higher SHAI → more never-disclosing (1.47).

Cyberchondria, more than health anxiety, is what goes with behaviour: urgent visits, a strained relationship and more disclosure.
Table 11 + Figure 7 · Pathways (mediation)
To test H3 (the cycle). A path diagram is the clearest way to show a, b and direct paths; the table adds the standard errors, the Sobel test and the reverse model.

How to read it:

  1. Boxes = variables; arrows = paths with coefficients.
  2. a = X → M, b = M → Y, c′ = direct.
  3. Below each diagram: the indirect effect with its Sobel z and p.

What it shows: Via excessive searching 0.16; via distress 0.22 (direct falls to 0.01); reverse also 0.15.

The pattern fits a cycle, but because the reverse direction also fits, we cannot say which comes first.
Never say “proves”.
Figure 8 · Facets by health-anxiety group
Shows simply that people with elevated health anxiety score higher on every facet, and which facets differ most. Bars with 95% CI are best for comparing means of two groups.

How to read it:

  1. Blue = SHAI < 18 (243 people); orange = SHAI ≥ 18 (57 people).
  2. Bar height = mean; small lines = 95% CI.

What it shows: Biggest gaps: excessiveness (9.2 vs 11.6) and distress (6.8 vs 9.2).

People with elevated health anxiety score higher on every part of cyberchondria, most of all on excessive searching and distress.
Supplementary figure · Item by item (slide 19 only)
To see which exact questions drive the link (168 pairs). Only a heatmap can show 168 numbers readably, so it goes in the appendix.

How to read it:

  1. Rows = 14 SHAI items; columns = 12 CSS items.
  2. Colour = strength of the Spearman correlation between two questions.

What it shows: 111/168 pairs significant; strongest: fear of serious illness ↔ panic on reading of a rare condition (0.43). Noticing aches is almost unrelated.

What drives searching is fear and interpretation, not simply noticing symptoms.
