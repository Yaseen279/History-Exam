This guide goes through the whole thesis, from the title page to the last reference. It assumes you know your subject and your data, but nothing about research methods or statistics. The study on one page

QuestionIs health anxiety linked to cyberchondria in adults in Baghdad, what role do AI chatbots play, and are medical students more affected?
DesignAnalytical cross-sectional online survey (Google Form, Arabic and English), convenience and snowball sampling, n = 300, one response per Google account, no missing data.

A2. Types of study design

DesignWhat it doesCan it show cause?
Cross-sectional (ours)Measures everything once, at one point in time, in one groupNo: shows associations only
Case–controlStarts from people with and without a disease and looks back for causesWeakly
Cohort (longitudinal)Follows the same people over timeBetter: shows what comes first
Randomised controlled trialRandomly gives an intervention to some peopleYes: the strongest
Why we did it this way

A cross-sectional survey was the only realistic design: we wanted to measure how common something is and how two things relate, in a few months, without money or a clinic. “Analytical” means we did not only describe, we also tested relationships (hypotheses).

A3. Population, sample and sampling

Population
everyone we want to talk about: adults in Baghdad who search for health information online.
Sample
the 300 people who actually answered.
Probability sampling
everyone has a known chance of being chosen (random). The results can be generalised. Needs a list of the whole population.
Convenience sampling (ours)
whoever is easy to reach (our social-media and messaging groups).
Snowball sampling (ours)
participants pass the link to others.
Why we did it this way

There is no list of all Baghdad adults who search online, so random sampling was impossible. Convenience and snowball sampling are standard for online student surveys. The price: percentages cannot be generalised to all of Baghdad, although associations (like r = 0.47) are still informative.

The type of variable decides the test. Score vs groups → Mann–Whitney/Kruskal–Wallis; category vs category → χ²; score vs score → correlation; many predictors → regression.

A5–A12 · The statistical ideas behind every number

A5. Hypotheses and the null hypothesis

A hypothesis is a prediction you test. Statistics always tests the opposite, the null hypothesis (H0): “there is no link / no difference”. If the data are very unlikely under H0, we reject it and accept our hypothesis.

H1 (ours): more health anxiety → more cyberchondria H0: no relationship between health anxiety and cyberchondria Result: r = 0.47, p < 0.001 → reject H0 → H1 supported

A6. The p-value

If there were really no effect, the p-value is the chance of seeing a result at least this strong by luck. p < 0.05 = significant (less than 5% chance of luck). It does not tell you how big or important the effect is.

A7. The 95% confidence interval (CI)

The range where the true value probably lies. Narrow = precise. For a difference or B: if the CI includes 0 → not significant. For an odds ratio: if it includes 1 → not significant.

A8. Effect size

How big the effect is: r (0.1 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large), Cramér’s V (same), R² (share explained), odds ratio (1 = none). A good thesis reports both the p-value and the effect size.

A9. Normal distribution, parametric and non-parametric tests

Many tests (t-test, ANOVA) assume scores form a symmetric bell curve. The Shapiro–Wilk test checks this. Ours were not normal, so we used non-parametric tests, which compare ranks instead of means, and describe groups with medians.

بالعربي

التوزيع الطبيعي هو شكل الجرس. درجاتنا لم تكن طبيعية، لذلك استخدمنا اختبارات لا معلمية (Mann–Whitney و Kruskal–Wallis) التي تعتمد على ترتيب القيم بدل المتوسطات.

A10. Reliability and validity

Reliability
does the scale measure consistently? Checked with Cronbach’s α (≥ 0.7 acceptable, ≥ 0.8 good).
Validity
does it measure what it claims? We used scales already validated in other studies (SHAI-14, CSS-12).
Our values
SHAI-14 α = 0.81, CSS-12 α = 0.86; the same in Arabic (0.81, 0.86) and English (0.81, 0.88).

A11. Adjusted versus unadjusted, and confounding

An unadjusted result looks at one variable alone. An adjusted result (regression) holds other variables constant. A difference can disappear after adjustment if a confounder explains it: medical students had higher health anxiety only because they were younger (H2).

The structured Abstract

Ours uses the standard medical format: Background · Objectives · Methods · Results · Conclusion · Keywords. Each heading is one or two sentences. Numbers in the Abstract must match the Results exactly.

Why we did it this way

A structured abstract is the format of medical journals and most college guidelines; it lets a reader find the design and main result in seconds. Keywords (cyberchondria; health anxiety; artificial intelligence; chatbots; Iraq) help indexing.

If they ask

Why is the title so long?

It contains the three variables, the population and the design, which is what a reader needs to judge the study at a glance.

Box 1

Box 1 separates four terms that overlap: health anxiety (a dimension, screened), illness anxiety disorder (a DSM-5-TR diagnosis, not assessed), cyberchondria (a behavioural pattern, measured with the CSS-12) and ordinary health searching (normal behaviour). It answers the question “is cyberchondria a disease?” (no: it is a pattern, not a diagnosis).

Aim, objectives and hypotheses

Aim
to study the relationship between health anxiety and cyberchondria among adults in Baghdad who search online, with a focus on AI chatbots and medical students.
Objective 1
describe where people look for health information and what they do after searching.
Objective 2
estimate levels of health anxiety and cyberchondria and the strength of their association, independently of other factors (H1).
Objective 3
compare medical students with other participants (H2).
H1 (primary)
people with more health anxiety have more cyberchondria, independently of socio-demographic and digital-use factors.
H2 (secondary)
medical students have higher health anxiety and cyberchondria than other participants.
Why we did it this way

Aim = the general purpose. Objectives = the concrete steps. Hypotheses = testable predictions. H1 comes from the literature (meta-analyses found a strong link); H2 comes from the idea of “medical student syndrome”. Writing hypotheses makes the study analytical, not just descriptive.

If they ask

Why only two hypotheses?

They are the two questions our data can test properly. Description (Objective 1) needs no hypothesis.

Is cyberchondria a disease?

No. It is a behavioural pattern measured on a scale, not a diagnosis. Box 1 explains the difference.

What is the gap your study fills?

No study from Baghdad, almost none on AI chatbots separately from search engines, and mixed evidence on medical students.

B3 · Chapter 2: Methods

2.1 Design, setting and participants

Design
analytical cross-sectional online survey (see A2).
Reporting guidelines
STROBE [23] (for observational studies) and CHERRIES [24] (for internet surveys): checklists of what to report so the study can be judged and repeated.
Sampling
convenience and snowball through Baghdad-based social-media and messaging groups (see A3).
Eligibility
adults ≥ 18, able to read Arabic or English, gave consent.
Duplicates
one response per Google account; no duplicate patterns; all submissions analysed (n = 300).
If they ask

Why online and not face to face?

Faster, cheaper, anonymous (people answer more honestly about anxiety), and the target group is people who search online anyway.

Why STROBE and CHERRIES?

They are the international checklists for observational and web surveys; following them shows we reported everything needed.

2.2 Sample size

To detect r = 0.20 with α = 0.05 (two-sided) and 80% power → 194 participants (Fisher’s z method) We had 300 → enough. Margin of error for a percentage with n = 300: about ±5.7%
Why we did it this way

We planned for a small correlation (0.20) to be safe; meta-analyses found 0.62, which needs far fewer people. Power = the chance of detecting a real effect (80% is the standard). α = the chance of a false positive we accept (5%).

بالعربي

حجم العينة المطلوب كان 194 شخصاً لاكتشاف علاقة صغيرة (0.20)، ونحن جمعنا 300، أي أكثر من الكافي.

2.3 The questionnaire

PartContent
1Consent and socio-demographic data (age, gender, education, study/work, chronic condition)
2Digital health-information seeking: tools (Q8), main AI chatbot (Q9), motives, trust (1–5)
3SHAI-14: 14 items, each 0–3, total 0–42 [26]; validated [27,28]
4CSS-12: 12 items, each 1–5, total 12–60, four 3-item dimensions [8]
5Behaviour after searching, telling the doctor, effect on the relationship
Why we did it this way
  • Why the SHAI-14? Short, widely used, good reliability, and it has a published cut-off (≥ 18) to identify elevated health anxiety.
  • Why the CSS-12? The short form of the 33-item CSS: much quicker but keeps all four dimensions (excessiveness, compulsion, distress, reassurance seeking).
  • Why bilingual? Many young Iraqis, especially medical students, read English; offering both increases participation.
  • Limitation: the team prepared the Arabic wording without formal back-translation [29]; published Arabic versions were not used [9,30]. α was the same in both languages, which supports the Arabic version.
If they ask

What is back-translation?

Translating the Arabic version back into English by a second translator and comparing, to check the meaning is the same. We did not do it; that is a limitation.

Why not the 33-item CSS?

Too long for an online survey (people drop out); the CSS-12 is validated and keeps the four dimensions.

If they ask

Which software?

IBM SPSS Statistics version 26, two-sided p < 0.05.

Why didn’t you correct for multiple testing?

Like most graduation theses we used p < 0.05; we state it as a limitation. The main results have p < 0.001.

2.6 Ethical considerations

Anonymous online survey of consenting adults, outside any hospital, no intervention, no medical records. The first page explained the study; electronic consent (and age ≥ 18) was required before any question; no names, e-mails or contact details were collected.

If they ask

Why didn’t you need ethics committee approval?

The supervisor confirmed it was not required for an anonymous, voluntary online survey of adults with no intervention and no patient records.

How did you protect participants?

Informed consent first, anonymity (no identifying data), voluntary participation, and they could stop at any time.

Post-hoc: which groups differ?

Kruskal–Wallis only says the groups are not all the same. Pairwise Mann–Whitney tests with Bonferroni correction show: 18–19 vs 30–39 (p = 0.026), current students vs bachelor graduates (p = 0.032), medical students vs non-health employees (p = 0.004). All point to the same thing: younger students are more health-anxious.

Table 7 · Yes/no outcomes (χ² or Fisher)

High cyberchondria with elevated health anxiety: 30/57 = 52.6% without: 56/243 = 23.0% Odds ratio = (30/27) / (56/187) = 1.11 / 0.30 = 3.71 (95% CI 2.04–6.76), χ² p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.26

Also significant: elevated health anxiety more common in reassurance seekers (27.5% vs 10.2%) and social-media users (40.6% vs 16.4%); high cyberchondria more common in AI users (31.2% vs 8.8%, p = 0.007) and in people who always tell their doctor (40.7% vs 15.8% of those who never do).

Part D

60 questions the committee may ask, with short model answers. Cover the answers and say yours out loud.

D · Design and sampling

Why a cross-sectional design?

It measures prevalence and associations at one time point, quickly and cheaply. It cannot show cause, which we state as a limitation.

Why not a cohort study?

It needs following people over months or years, beyond a graduation project. We recommend it for future research.

Why convenience and snowball sampling?

No list of all Baghdad adults who search online exists, so random sampling was impossible.

Can you generalise your results?

Percentages, no: the sample is young and online. Associations, yes, with caution.

Why so many medical students?

The link spread through our own networks first. After adjustment, being a medical student did not change scores.

How did you prevent duplicate answers?

One response per Google account, and no duplicate patterns were found.

Why is the sample 300?

194 were needed to detect r = 0.20; 300 gives more power and a ±5.7% margin for percentages.

D · Questionnaire and variables

Why the SHAI-14?

Short, validated, reliable, with a published cut-off (≥ 18).

Why ≥ 18?

It best separated people with health anxiety from healthy controls in the Swedish validation study.

Why the CSS-12?

Validated short form of the 33-item CSS keeping all four dimensions.

What are the four CSS dimensions?

Excessiveness (searching a lot), compulsion (it disrupts life), distress (worse after searching), reassurance seeking (consulting doctors because of it).

Did you translate the scales formally?

No back-translation; a limitation. Reliability was the same in Arabic and English.

How did you define an AI user?

Ticked “AI chatbots” or named a chatbot; the 32 who named one without ticking were counted as users because they use it.

Why is high cyberchondria > 33?

No validated cut-off exists for the CSS-12, so we used the upper third.

Why merge ages 40+?

Each of the oldest groups was too small to analyse alone.

D · Statistics

Why non-parametric tests?

Shapiro–Wilk showed the scores were not normal.

Why no ANOVA or t-test?

Same reason; Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney are their non-parametric versions. We checked: the conclusions are the same.

Why medians?

Rank-based tests go with medians; for skewed data the median describes a typical person better.

Which groups differ in Table 6?

Post-hoc: 18–19 vs 30–39, students vs graduates, medical students vs non-health employees; younger students are more anxious.

Why both Pearson and Spearman?

Pearson is standard; Spearman confirms it without assuming normality; both were 0.47.

What does partial r mean?

The correlation after removing the effect of other variables: 0.48, unchanged.

Why hierarchical regression?

To show how much health anxiety adds after demographics and digital use: 18.4%.

Difference between B and β?

B is in real units (CSS points per unit); β is standardised so predictors can be compared.

Why logistic regression?

The outcome (elevated health anxiety) is yes/no.

What is an AOR?

Adjusted odds ratio: how many times the odds change with other factors held constant.

What is Cramér’s V?

The strength of a χ² association (0.1 small, 0.3 medium).

Why Fisher’s exact test?

χ² is unreliable when an expected count is below 5.

What is the AUC?

How well the model separates the two groups; 0.83 is good.

What does Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.50 mean?

The model fits well (we want p > 0.05).

Why not correct for multiple testing?

Standard p < 0.05; stated as a limitation; main results p < 0.001.

Which software?

IBM SPSS Statistics 26.

D · Results and interpretation

What is your main finding?

Health anxiety and cyberchondria are moderately associated, r = 0.47, independently of other factors.

What does r = 0.47 mean in plain words?

People who worry more about illness search more and more anxiously; worry explains about 22% of the differences.

Does health anxiety cause cyberchondria?

We cannot say; one survey cannot show direction. A Dutch study suggests they feed each other.

Why is your r lower than the meta-analyses (0.62)?

Low, narrow health anxiety in our sample, confident internet users, and the short CSS-12.

Is 19% a lot?

It is a screening estimate; at stricter cut-offs it is 7.3% (≥ 22) and 0.7% (≥ 29). It shows a substantial minority with elevated worry.

What about AI users?

They had higher cyberchondria, 5.3 points, independently of health anxiety (p < 0.001). The non-user group was small, so it needs confirmation.

Why might AI increase cyberchondria?

Chatbots can be asked again and again, encouraging repeated searching and reassurance seeking.

Why was H2 not supported?

Medical students’ higher health anxiety disappeared after adjusting for age; their cyberchondria was identical.

Is “medical student syndrome” real?

Not in our sample: medical knowledge neither increased nor protected against cyberchondria.

Why does age matter in the regression but not in Table 6?

Older people had lower anxiety but similar searching; holding anxiety constant reveals their relatively higher searching.

Why do 34.7% delay care?

Reassuring information online; it may save visits for minor symptoms but risks late diagnosis.

Why do people hide searches from the doctor?

Mainly fear of being judged; hidden information can strain the relationship.

D · Ethics, limitations and impact

Was ethics approval needed?

The supervisor confirmed not, for an anonymous voluntary online survey of adults with no intervention.

How was consent obtained?

Electronic consent, with age ≥ 18 confirmed, before any question.

What is the most important limitation?

The cross-sectional design: no direction can be shown.

What would you do differently?

A probability sample, a formally translated Arabic version, and a longitudinal design.

What is new in your study?

First CSS-12 study in Baghdad; AI chatbots separate from search engines; medical students compared with adjustment.

What should doctors do?

Ask without judgement what patients searched or asked an AI, and clear up misunderstandings.

Who benefits from your study?

Clinicians, medical educators, the Ministry of Health (health-literacy campaigns) and AI developers.
